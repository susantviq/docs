> The percentage of your GL postings created as manual journal entries rather than system-generated transactions. A high share signals control weakness or broken automation.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> The share of your general-ledger activity that comes from manual journal entries, as opposed to transactions the system generated automatically (cash sales, invoices, vendor bills, payments). It is a control-quality gauge: a healthy, well-automated finance function posts almost everything through transactional flows and uses manual journals sparingly for genuine adjustments. When the manual share climbs, it usually means automation has broken and people are patching the books by hand, or that controls have loosened and manual overrides have become routine.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | Manual journal entries as a percentage of all GL-posting transactions over the window. The denominator is total posting transactions; the numerator is the manual-journal subset. |
| **What it measures** | Control quality and automation health. Lower is generally better; the right floor is non-zero because legitimate adjustments always exist. |
| **Currency** | n/a. The headline is a count-based percentage; manual journals can be read by value separately. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Respects dashboard filter. On OneWorld, the share can be read per subsidiary. |
| **Time window** | `30D` (trailing 30-day share) |
| **Alert trigger** | Share greater than 25% |
| **Roles** | owner, finance |
| **Chart type** | Gauge (percentage), banded, with the manual-JE count and total-posting count beneath |
{/* /HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}

## Calculation

Calculated automatically from your NetSuite data. The card counts manual journal entries posted over the trailing 30-day window and divides by the total count of GL-posting transactions in the same window, expressing the result as a percentage. It deliberately separates manual journals (entries a person created by hand to record an adjustment, accrual, reclassification or correction) from system-generated postings (the cash sales, invoices, fulfilments, bills and payments that flow automatically from operational events).

The card is a control gauge, not a business-performance metric. A low, stable share is the signature of a clean, automated finance function; a rising share is one of the earliest signals that automation has broken or that manual overrides are becoming a habit. The alert fires above 25%, a level at which manual postings have become a material part of the ledger rather than the exception. See the At a glance summary above and the worked example below for how to interpret a reading.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A US food-and-beverage brand on NetSuite OneWorld. US parent (USD) runs DTC plus grocery wholesale; a UK sub (GBP) runs a smaller DTC operation. A marketplace settlement integration normally posts settlements automatically. Annual revenue ~$40M. 30-day window 13 May 26 to 12 Jun 26. Reporting currency USD.

| Posting source | Count | Share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System-generated (cash sales, invoices, fulfilments, bills, payments) | 9,650 | 78% |
| Manual journal entries | 2,720 | 22% |
| **Total GL-posting transactions** | **12,370** | **100%** |
| Of which manual JEs are settlement corrections | 1,900 | 70% of manual JEs |
| Of which manual JEs are genuine adjustments | 820 | 30% of manual JEs |

Five things to notice:

1. **At 22% the gauge is amber and approaching the 25% alert, and that proximity is itself the signal.** A manual-JE share in the low-20s is not yet a crisis, but it is well above where a clean, automated mid-market finance function should sit, which is typically in the single digits or low teens. The gauge sitting just under the threshold is a warning that the next process slip will tip it into the red. The whole point of a 25% line is to fire before manual posting becomes the dominant mode of recording the books; by the time a quarter of all GL activity is hand-keyed, the finance function has effectively stopped trusting its own automation. Reading 22% means the team should be asking why the manual share is this high now, not waiting for it to cross 25%.

2. **The breakdown is the entire story: 70% of the manual journals are settlement corrections, which means automation is broken, not that controls are loose.** The card headline of 22% is ambiguous on its own, because a high manual share can mean two very different things: either people are deliberately overriding controls (a governance problem) or an automated process has broken and humans are patching the gap (an integration problem). Here the breakdown is decisive: 1,900 of the 2,720 manual journals are settlement corrections, meaning the marketplace settlement integration that should post automatically has stopped working, and the finance team is hand-keying every settlement. The fix is not a controls memo, it is an engineering ticket to repair the settlement integration, which would drop the manual share from 22% straight back into single digits.

3. **The 820 genuine adjustments are the irreducible floor, and the gauge should never target zero.** Real businesses always need some manual journals: month-end accruals, prepaid amortisation, depreciation, reclassifications, intercompany true-ups, and one-off corrections that no automation should be expected to handle. These 820 entries are the healthy baseline. If you stripped out the broken-settlement corrections, the manual share would fall to roughly 8%, which is exactly where a well-run finance function lives. The lesson is that the target is not zero manual journals but zero unnecessary manual journals; the gauge is high here entirely because of the avoidable 1,900, not the legitimate 820.

4. **High manual-JE share and transaction imbalances are dangerous companions.** Manual journals are the single largest source of hand-keyed errors, because a person entering debits and credits by hand can transpose a figure, miss a line, or misclassify an account. The more of your GL activity is manual, the more opportunities exist for a posting to fail the debit-equals-credit rule and be rejected, which is exactly what the [Transaction Imbalances](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/transaction-imbalances) card detects. A 22% manual share alongside a non-zero imbalance count is a strong signal that the hand-keying volume has outrun the team's accuracy. Cutting the manual share by fixing the broken automation also cuts the imbalance risk, because there is simply less hand entry to get wrong.

5. **On OneWorld, a single broken integration on one sub can drive the consolidated share even when the other subs are clean.** Here the broken settlement integration lives on the US parent, so the US sub's manual share is high while the UK sub may be sitting at a healthy 6%. The consolidated 22% blends them and makes the whole group look like it has a control problem, when in reality one entity has one broken integration. Read the gauge per subsidiary using the dashboard filter to localise the issue, because the remediation (repair one integration on one sub) is far narrower than the consolidated number implies. The consolidated gauge raises the question; the per-sub read answers where to act.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

NetSuite distinguishes journal entries from system-generated transactions cleanly, so this card reconciles directly against native transaction reporting. The closest views:

> **Transactions → Financial → Make Journal Entries → List** (all journal entries over the window, the numerator)
> **Transactions → Management → Transaction Saved Search** grouped by Type, counting all posting transactions (the denominator)
> **Reports → Financial → General Ledger** filtered to journal-entry source, to see what the manual entries actually posted to
> **Reports → Audit Trail / System Notes** to confirm which journals were hand-created versus generated by a script or recurring template
> **Saved search**: a transaction saved search counting `Journal` type against total posting transactions, which is the calculation this card performs

The headline percentage should tie out closely to a manual count from these views. The most common reconciliation nuance is which journals count as "manual", covered below, because NetSuite can generate journal entries automatically (recurring templates, SuiteScript, system revaluation) that are not hand-keyed and should arguably be excluded from the manual numerator.

**Why our number may legitimately differ from a manual count:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Script-generated journals** | Either | A journal posted by SuiteScript or a scheduled process is technically a journal entry but is not hand-keyed. Whether the card counts these as manual changes the numerator significantly on automated accounts. |
| **Recurring/memorised journals** | Either | Memorised recurring journals (depreciation, standard accruals) post automatically from a template; counting them as manual overstates the control concern. |
| **System revaluation entries** | Card may read lower | FX revaluation and other system-posted journals are journals by type but are automation, not manual override; the card typically excludes them from the manual numerator. |
| **Denominator scope** | Either | Whether the denominator is all posting transactions or only financial-impact transactions changes the percentage; a saved search with a different scope will differ. |
| **Posting vs non-posting** | Either | Non-posting transactions (sales orders, quotes) in the denominator dilute the percentage; excluding them raises it. |
| **Window boundaries** | Small | The 30-day trailing window versus a fixed saved-search date range can cut the boundary differently. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Either | Consolidated versus per-sub reads differ sharply when one sub carries most of the manual entries. |

**Cross-connector reconciliation:**

The manual-JE share is a control signal that becomes far more meaningful when read against the commerce automation that should be replacing manual work. The promise of an integrated commerce-plus-ERP stack is that operational events post automatically; a high manual share is evidence that promise is not being kept somewhere.

| Card | Expected relationship | What the comparison reveals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Commerce Orders Without a NetSuite SO](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/commerce-orders-without-netsuite-so) | Common root cause | When order automation breaks, finance often records the missing revenue via manual journals, so both cards rise together; the manual share is the symptom, the missing SOs are the cause. |
| [Transactions by Type](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/transactions-by-type) | Containing view | The JE segment of the type donut is the numerator of this gauge; a swelling JE segment and a rising manual share are the same signal at different resolutions. |
| [shopify.orders](/nerve-centre/shopify/orders) | Inverse signal | A high manual share alongside healthy Shopify order volume suggests orders are being recorded by hand instead of flowing through automation. |
| [Transaction Imbalances](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/transaction-imbalances) | Compounding risk | More manual entry means more chances for a hand-keyed imbalance; the two cards together quantify manual-process risk. |

The killer cross-channel framing is that a high manual-JE share is often the accounting shadow of a broken commerce integration. When the storefront keeps selling but the integration stops posting, the orders do not vanish from the business, they get hand-keyed into the GL as manual journals so the books still balance at month end. NetSuite alone sees a lot of manual journals and might conclude the finance team simply prefers manual control. Only by joining the manual-JE share to the commerce order volume and the orders-without-SO count does the real story appear: the team is manually replacing a broken automation, transaction by transaction. That join turns a vague control-quality concern into a specific, fixable integration defect, which is the kind of root-cause that no single system can surface alone.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**Why is a high manual-JE share a problem?**
Because manual journals bypass the controls, validations and audit trail that system-generated transactions enforce. A cash sale carries a customer, items, tax and a clear operational origin; a manual journal is just debits and credits a person typed, with whatever memo they chose to write. The more of your ledger is built from manual journals, the less of it is automatically validated, the harder it is to audit, and the more it depends on individual people remembering to do the right thing. A high share is a control-quality red flag even when every individual entry is correct.

**What is a healthy manual-JE share?**
For a well-automated mid-market commerce business, single digits to low teens is healthy. Some manual journals are always legitimate: accruals, prepaid amortisation, depreciation, reclassifications, intercompany true-ups and genuine corrections. The 25% alert is set where manual posting has become a material part of the ledger rather than the exception. If you are consistently above the teens, the question is which automated process is missing or broken, because a clean stack should not need a quarter of its postings keyed by hand.

**Does the card distinguish broken automation from loose controls?**
The headline does not, but the breakdown beneath it usually does. A manual share that is dominated by one repeating category (settlement corrections, missing-order revenue) points at a broken automation that humans are patching. A manual share spread across many varied categories points at a culture of manual override or weak process discipline. The two have different fixes (an engineering ticket versus a controls intervention), so always read the composition of the manual journals, not just the percentage.

**Should I be counting script-generated journals as manual?**
Generally no, and this is the most important configuration question for the card. A journal posted by SuiteScript, a recurring memorised template, or system FX revaluation is a journal entry by record type but is not hand-keyed and does not carry the control risk of a manual override. The card typically excludes these from the manual numerator so the gauge measures genuine human entry. If your reading looks surprisingly high, the first check is whether automated journals are being counted as manual.

**OneWorld and subsidiaries, how should I read the share?**
Read it per subsidiary to localise the cause. A single broken integration on one sub can drive the consolidated share up while the other subs are clean, making the whole group look like it has a control problem when it has one fixable integration on one entity. Intercompany journals are also a legitimate manual category that concentrates on OneWorld accounts, so a OneWorld group will naturally carry a slightly higher baseline manual share than a single-entity business. Account for the intercompany floor before reading the gauge as a control concern.

**FX and multi-currency, does it affect this card?**
The count-based headline is currency-agnostic, because it counts journals regardless of currency. The nuance is that multi-currency businesses generate more legitimate manual and system journals (FX revaluation, intercompany true-ups, currency reclassifications), which raises the baseline. System FX revaluation journals should be excluded from the manual numerator; if they are not, a multi-currency account will read artificially high. Confirm the revaluation journals are classified as automation, not manual.

**B2B versus DTC, does the share read differently?**
Somewhat. DTC flows are highly automated (checkout to cash sale to fulfilment), so a clean DTC business should have a low manual share, and any elevation is a loud signal. B2B involves more invoicing, terms management, credit handling and customer-specific arrangements, some of which legitimately require manual journals, so a B2B book carries a slightly higher healthy baseline. A hybrid business sits between. Read the threshold against your model: a DTC brand at 22% is more alarming than a complex B2B distributor at 22%.

**Does period close cause a spike?**
Yes, and it is the most important context for reading this card. Close periods are when accruals, reversals, prepaid amortisation, depreciation and reclassifications all post, and most of those are manual or template journals. A manual-JE share that spikes during close week and settles afterward is entirely normal and expected. The signal to act on is a manual share that stays elevated outside the close window, because that is structural manual posting, not the legitimate rhythm of the accounting calendar.

**How does this relate to transaction imbalances?**
Manual journals are the leading source of hand-keyed imbalances, because a person typing debits and credits can transpose, omit or misclassify. A high manual share raises the statistical chance of an imbalance, so the two cards are companions: cutting unnecessary manual entry reduces both the control concern and the imbalance risk. If you see a rising manual share and a non-zero imbalance count together, the manual-entry workflow is the common cause.

**Can the gauge be high for a legitimate reason that I should not fix?**
Occasionally, yes. A business mid-implementation, mid-acquisition-integration, or running a temporary manual process while an automation is being built will legitimately show a high manual share for a defined period. The card cannot tell a temporary, known, controlled manual process from an unmanaged one. The judgement is yours: if the elevated share is explained by a known project with an end date, it is acceptable; if it is unexplained and persistent, it is the control problem the alert is designed to catch.

**NetSuite versus other ERPs, is this control metric portable?**
Entirely. The ratio of manual journals to system-generated postings is a universal internal-controls measure that auditors look at across every ERP. SAP S/4HANA and Oracle ERP Cloud both distinguish manually-entered journals from automated postings, and the same interpretation applies: a low manual share signals a clean, automated, auditable finance function, and a rising share signals broken automation or loosening control. The NetSuite specifics are the record-type definitions and the SuiteScript-versus-manual distinction; the control principle is identical regardless of platform, which is why this is one of the cards external auditors most readily understand.
