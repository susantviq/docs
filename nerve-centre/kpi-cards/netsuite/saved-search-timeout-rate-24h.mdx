> Saved Search Timeout Rate (24h): the percentage of Saved Search executions that hit NetSuite's processing timeout over the last 24 hours, a health gauge for the queries finance and integrations depend on.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> What share of your Saved Search runs failed to finish before NetSuite's processing timeout in the last 24 hours. Saved Searches power the morning finance review, the dashboards, and the integrations that read data out of NetSuite, so when they time out, queries return nothing and the people and systems relying on them are blocked. A creeping timeout rate is an early warning that searches need tuning, filters need tightening, or the account's data has outgrown how the searches were written. Because it gates whether finance can actually pull its numbers, this is a Hero card.

## Calculation

The card expresses, as a percentage, how many Saved Search executions in the last 24 hours hit NetSuite's processing timeout rather than completing. The numerator is the count of timed-out executions; the denominator is the total executions in the rolling window. A reading of 2% means one execution in fifty failed to finish; a reading of 12% means roughly one in eight queries returned nothing. Because the card is a rate, it normalises across busy and quiet days, so a spike reflects a genuine deterioration in query health rather than just more traffic.

The card does not identify which specific search timed out or why; it reports the aggregate health so the team knows when to investigate. Timeouts have a small set of usual causes: a search scanning too much data because its filters are too broad, a search with expensive formula columns or joins, an account whose data volume has grown past what the search was written to handle, or contention when many heavy searches run at once (often the morning batch). Because Saved Searches underpin so much, the morning finance review, operational dashboards, and the integrations that extract data, a rising timeout rate is an early-warning signal that something needs tuning before the people and systems that depend on those queries are blocked.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A multi-brand commerce group on NetSuite OneWorld. Four subsidiaries, roughly $130M annual revenue, with a heavy reliance on Saved Searches: a 7am scheduled batch that refreshes finance dashboards, several integration searches that feed external reporting, and ad-hoc searches the finance team runs during the morning review. Snapshot covers the 24 hours to 09 Jun 26, 09:00 EST. These figures are illustrative.

| Execution source | Executions (24h) | Timeouts | Timeout rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7am scheduled dashboard batch | 180 | 22 | 12.2% |
| Integration extract searches | 640 | 9 | 1.4% |
| Ad-hoc finance searches | 410 | 31 | 7.6% |
| Other / background | 770 | 4 | 0.5% |
| **Total** | **2,000** | **66** | **3.3%** |

Five things to notice:

1. **The blended rate of 3.3% sits below the 5% alert, yet two specific sources are badly degraded, which is the first lesson of reading an aggregate rate.** The total looks acceptable, but it averages a healthy background (0.5%) and a low-volume integration tier (1.4%) against two genuinely sick sources: the 7am dashboard batch at 12.2% and ad-hoc finance searches at 7.6%. The high-volume healthy traffic dilutes the rate below the threshold, so the headline gauge stays green while the finance team's actual experience, the morning review, is broken. This is why the breakdown matters more than the headline: a green aggregate can hide a red workflow. A team relying only on the top-line gauge would conclude all is well while the controller cannot load the morning dashboard.

2. **The 7am batch timing out at 12.2% is the single most damaging line, because it is exactly the data finance needs first and exactly when contention peaks.** Scheduled batches that all fire at the same early-morning slot compete for the same processing capacity, so heavy searches that complete fine in isolation start timing out when run shoulder to shoulder. The result is that one query in eight in the morning refresh returns nothing, so dashboards load with gaps and the controller starts the day chasing missing numbers instead of reviewing them. The fix is usually operational before it is technical: stagger the batch so heavy searches do not all run at 7am, and only then tune the individual searches. The card surfaces the symptom; the timing of the spike points straight at contention as the likely cause.

3. **Ad-hoc finance searches at 7.6% reveal a different failure mode, queries written for convenience rather than efficiency.** Unlike the scheduled batch, ad-hoc searches are run by people, often with broad date ranges, no subsidiary filter, or expensive formula columns added on the fly to answer a one-off question. These are individually slow and frequently time out, especially when run against a OneWorld account's full data volume. The fix here is enablement, not just engineering: teaching the finance team to scope searches (tighter date ranges, subsidiary filters, fewer formula columns) and providing pre-built efficient searches for common questions. A 7.6% ad-hoc timeout rate is a sign the team is fighting the data rather than the data being fundamentally too big.

4. **Because this is a Hero card, the right reading is "can finance do its job this morning", not just "is the number under 5%".** A timed-out search returns no data, so the downstream effect is invisible failure: a dashboard tile shows blank or stale, an integration extract delivers a short file, a report runs empty. None of these throw an obvious alarm on the financial cards themselves; they just quietly under-deliver. The timeout rate is the one place this class of failure is visible before someone notices a missing number. That is why it earns Hero status despite being an operational rather than a financial metric: it gates the reliability of the entire data layer that every other card and every morning review sits on top of.

5. **Data volume growth means a search that was healthy last quarter can cross into timeout territory with no code change at all, which is why the trend matters as much as the level.** As the account accumulates transactions, customers, and items, a Saved Search that scanned a manageable dataset a year ago now scans far more, and at some point it crosses the processing budget and starts timing out. Nothing in the search changed; the data outgrew it. This is the most insidious cause because there is no deployment to blame and no obvious trigger, just a slow creep in the rate over weeks. Watching this card's trend, not just today's value, is how you catch a search aging into failure before it tips over the threshold. The single-day 3.3% is less informative than the fact that it was 1% three months ago.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

Saved Search execution and performance are observable in NetSuite, so this card triangulates against the search-performance and logging tooling:

> **Reports → Saved Searches → All Saved Searches** (the inventory of searches; the heavy ones are the timeout candidates)
> **Search Performance / Performance Details** on individual searches (NetSuite surfaces execution time and performance metadata where available)
> **Customization → Scripting → Script Execution Log** (when a SuiteScript calls a search that times out, the failure trail lands here)
> **Application Performance Management (APM)** and the Performance SuiteApp / Page Time and Operation logs (the native performance-monitoring tooling where slow and timed-out operations are recorded)
> **Saved search**: a search over execution or performance logs filtered to timed-out operations within the last 24 hours, over total executions, is essentially the rate this card computes

The headline difference is that NetSuite exposes per-search and per-operation performance data, but it does not, on its own, roll those up into a single 24-hour timeout rate or alert you when it crosses a threshold. The card aggregates the executions, computes the rate, and applies the gauge and alert on top of NetSuite's raw performance signals.

**Why our rate may legitimately differ from NetSuite's performance logs:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What counts as a timeout** | Either | The card counts executions terminated by the processing timeout; a performance log may also include slow-but-completed runs that the card does not count as timeouts. |
| **Execution sources in scope** | Either | The card blends scheduled, integration, UI, and background executions; a log filtered to one source reads differently. |
| **Window boundary** | Small | The rolling 24-hour window can include or exclude executions near the boundary versus a log query using a fixed cut-off. |
| **Concurrency-related failures** | Either | Some failures are governance or concurrency limits rather than pure timeouts; depending on classification, these may or may not be counted as timeouts. |
| **Cached versus fresh runs** | Either | A search served from cache completes instantly and counts as a success; the same search run fresh might time out, so the rate depends on the cache mix. |
| **Account-level versus search-level** | Either | The card reports an account-wide rate; a single search's performance page reflects only that search, which can be far higher or lower than the blend. |
| **Sandbox versus production** | Either | Sandbox data volume and processing capacity differ from production, so timeout rates measured in a sandbox do not predict production behaviour. |

There is no cross-connector reconciliation for this card, because Saved Search timeout rate is a NetSuite platform-health concept with no equivalent in commerce or ad systems. Its value is internal: it is the early-warning gauge for the query layer that every other NetSuite card and every integration extract depends on. The only meaningful comparison is against NetSuite's own performance tooling, and the card's job is to turn that scattered per-search data into one timely, alertable rate.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**What is a Saved Search timeout, in plain terms?**
A Saved Search that runs too long and gets terminated by NetSuite's processing governance before it returns results. Instead of data, the caller, a user, a dashboard tile, or an integration, gets an error or an empty result. The card measures what share of all search executions in the last 24 hours ended this way. A timeout is silent in the sense that it does not corrupt data; it just delivers nothing, which is why it is easy to miss until a number turns up missing.

**Why is even a few percent a problem?**
Because the searches that time out are usually the heavy, important ones, the morning dashboard refresh, the close-reconciliation queries, the integration extracts, and a single failed run blocks a real workflow. A 5% blended rate can mean the most critical search of the day fails routinely while a thousand trivial searches succeed and flatter the average. The default alert at greater than 5% is a starting point; if a key workflow is sensitive, tune it lower.

**Why is this a Hero card if it is not a financial number?**
Because it gates whether finance can pull its numbers at all. Saved Searches underpin the dashboards, the morning review, and the integrations that read data out of NetSuite. When they time out, the downstream effect is invisible failure: blank tiles, short extracts, empty reports, none of which throw an obvious alarm on the financial cards. This rate is the one place that class of failure is visible early, so it protects the reliability of the entire data layer, which makes it high-value.

**What usually causes the rate to rise?**
Four common causes: searches with filters too broad so they scan too much data; searches with expensive formula columns or joins; account data volume growing past what the search was written to handle; and contention when many heavy searches run at the same time (classically the early-morning batch). Telling them apart needs the breakdown, contention spikes at a specific time, while data-volume growth shows as a slow creep over weeks with no trigger.

**A search that was fine last quarter now times out, with no change, why?**
Almost always data-volume growth. As the account accumulates transactions, customers, and items, a search that scanned a manageable dataset now scans far more and crosses the processing budget. Nothing in the search changed; the data outgrew it. This is why the trend matters as much as today's value: watching the rate creep up over weeks lets you re-tune a search before it tips over the threshold and breaks a workflow.

**How do I actually fix timeouts?**
Tune the searches. Tighten filters and date ranges so they scan less, reduce or remove expensive formula columns, add criteria that let NetSuite use indexed fields, and split very large searches into smaller scoped ones. For contention, stagger scheduled batches so heavy searches do not all run at once. For ad-hoc timeouts, provide the team with pre-built efficient searches and coach them to scope their own. The card tells you the rate is high; the per-search performance tooling tells you which search and why.

**OneWorld and subsidiary scope, does it matter here?**
Searches run against the account's data, and a OneWorld account simply has more data, so timeouts are more likely than on a single-entity account. A search scoped to one subsidiary scans less and is faster; an unscoped search across all subs scans everything and is a prime timeout candidate. Encouraging subsidiary-scoped searches where the question only concerns one entity is one of the most effective ways to bring the rate down on a large OneWorld account.

**Does this card see integration searches as well as user searches?**
Yes. The rate blends scheduled, integration, UI, and background executions, because all of them depend on the same processing capacity and any of them can time out. Integration extract searches are often the highest-volume source, so they can dominate the denominator; if you want to isolate the finance-review experience specifically, look at the breakdown by source rather than the blended rate.

**Could a high rate cause stuck transactions or a stalled close?**
Yes, indirectly, which is why the close and transaction cards are siblings. A SuiteScript that calls a search which times out can fail to complete its work, leaving transactions unposted or unapproved. A close-reconciliation search that times out can stall the reconciliation step. So a timeout spike can be the upstream technical cause behind a downstream symptom like a stuck approval queue or an overdue close. When those appear together, the search timeout is often the root.

**Does period close make this worse?**
Often, yes. Close is search-heavy: reconciliation queries, period-end reports, and validation searches all run in a concentrated window while the team is also running ad-hoc searches to chase variances. That concentration raises contention and pushes the timeout rate up exactly when reliable queries matter most. Watching this card during the close, and pre-tuning the heaviest close searches, is good close hygiene.

**How does this compare to query performance in other ERPs or data warehouses?**
Every system that lets you query large datasets has a performance ceiling, but the model differs. NetSuite governs Saved Search execution with processing limits and timeouts to protect shared capacity. A dedicated data warehouse would instead scale compute or queue queries rather than time them out. The card normalises NetSuite's timeout behaviour into a single health rate, so the reading is consistent for NetSuite; the remedies (tune the search, scope it, stagger the schedule) are NetSuite-specific and differ from how you would tune a warehouse query.
