> AP Aging 60+ Days: the share of accounts-payable balance aged 60 or more days, a vendor-relationship and working-capital signal.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> This is the percentage of everything you owe your vendors that has been sitting unpaid for 60 days or longer. A small, stable share is normal. A rising share means bills are slipping past terms, which strains vendor relationships, risks late fees and credit holds from suppliers, and is often the first visible symptom of a cash-flow squeeze. The card reads as a gauge so you can see at a glance whether your oldest payables are a healthy sliver or a growing problem.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | The proportion of total open accounts-payable balance whose age is 60 days or more, expressed as a percentage. Numerator is AP aged 60+; denominator is total open AP. |
| **Tax treatment** | n/a directly. The aging is on gross payable balances; any input-tax recovery sits on the underlying bills, not on the aging percentage. |
| **Currency** | Reporting currency. Foreign-currency vendor bills are translated to the reporting currency for the aging calculation. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Respects dashboard filter. On OneWorld, each subsidiary has its own AP; the percentage aggregates across subsidiaries in scope. |
| **Time window** | `30D vsP` (current 30-day reading compared against the prior period). |
| **Alert trigger** | Fires when the 60+ share rises `above 30%`. Tune to your payment-terms profile. |
| **Roles** | owner, finance |
| **Chart type** | Gauge (percentage, with prior-period comparison). |
| **Why it matters** | Aging payables strain vendor relationships and risk supply disruption, while also signalling whether the business is managing or merely stretching its cash. |
{/* /HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}

## Calculation

The card takes the total open accounts-payable balance and splits it by age, then expresses the portion aged 60 days or more as a percentage of the whole. Age is measured from the bill date or the due date depending on workspace configuration, and only open (unpaid or partially paid) bills count toward both the numerator and the denominator. Because it is a ratio, the card normalises for business size: a fast-growing company with a larger absolute AP balance can still show a healthy 60+ share, and a smaller company can show an alarming share even on a modest balance. The gauge presentation makes the read instant, and the prior-period comparison shows the direction of travel, which often matters more than the level.

The logic deliberately uses 60 days as the threshold because it sits past the typical 30-day net terms most vendors offer, so anything in the 60+ bucket is, for most suppliers, genuinely overdue rather than simply within terms. The card does not judge whether a given late payment is deliberate (stretching a cooperative supplier) or accidental (a stuck approval or a cash shortfall); it surfaces the share and the trend so finance can investigate the composition. The default alert at 30% is a generic ceiling; the right threshold depends on your vendor mix and payment-terms profile, which is why the card supports tuning per workspace.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A mid-market homewares brand on NetSuite OneWorld. Two subsidiaries: a US parent (USD) and a UK sub (GBP). Annual revenue roughly $48M. Current 30-day reading 14 Mar 26, compared against the prior period. Reporting currency USD. These figures are illustrative.

| AP aging bucket | Balance (USD) | Share of total AP |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current (not yet due) | 1,920,000 | 48% |
| 1-30 days | 880,000 | 22% |
| 31-60 days | 400,000 | 10% |
| 61-90 days | 520,000 | 13% |
| 91+ days | 280,000 | 7% |
| **Total open AP** | **4,000,000** | **100%** |
| **AP Aging 60+ (this card)** | **800,000** | **20%** |

Five things to notice:

1. **At 20%, this business is below the 30% alert ceiling, but the prior-period comparison is what tells the real story.** A 20% reading is comfortable in isolation, but if the prior period was 14% and it is now 20%, the share has grown by nearly half in one period, and that direction of travel is the signal worth acting on long before the absolute number crosses any threshold. The gauge level answers "how bad is it now"; the prior-period delta answers "where is this heading". A business that catches a rising 60+ share early can fix the cause while it is small; one that waits for the absolute level to breach 30% is reacting to a problem that has already strained vendor relationships. This is why the card pairs the level with the trend rather than showing the level alone.

2. **The 91+ bucket at 7% is more dangerous than its size suggests, because the oldest payables are where relationships break.** A bill that is 91 days overdue is well past the point where most vendors stay quiet. By then a supplier has typically sent reminders, may have applied late fees, and is increasingly likely to put the account on credit hold, which stops future orders. For a homewares brand heading into a seasonal buy, a single core supplier placing the account on hold can disrupt the entire range. The 60+ headline rolls the 61-90 and 91+ buckets together, but finance should always look at the 91+ slice separately, because it is the part of the balance most likely to convert from a working-capital choice into a supply-chain emergency.

3. **A rising 60+ share is not always a cash problem, and distinguishing the cause is the whole job.** There are three common stories behind a higher reading. The benign one is deliberate, negotiated stretching: paying cooperative suppliers slower to preserve cash, with their knowledge and consent, which is legitimate working-capital management. The operational one is a process failure: bills stuck in an approval queue, a disputed invoice nobody resolved, or a supplier-statement mismatch that froze payment. The dangerous one is a genuine cash shortfall: the business cannot pay on time because the money is not there. The card surfaces the share; the composition (which vendors, whether bills are approved-but-unpaid versus stuck-in-approval) tells you which story you are in. Pairing with the pending-approvals card separates "stuck process" from "no cash" immediately.

4. **On OneWorld, an aggregate 20% can hide one subsidiary in distress.** If the US parent runs a healthy 12% 60+ share but the UK sub is at 38%, the consolidated 20% looks acceptable while the UK sub is actually in trouble with its suppliers. Subsidiaries often have separate AP teams, separate banking, and separate cash positions, so a group-level percentage can smooth over a single entity that is stretching dangerously. Always drill the gauge by subsidiary before concluding the group is healthy. The most useful operational pattern is a consolidated reading for the board plus per-subsidiary readings for the AP managers who actually control payment timing.

5. **The 60+ share is the AP mirror of the AR aging story, and reading them together frames the working-capital cycle.** A business that is collecting slowly from its customers (high AR aging) and consequently paying slowly to its vendors (high AP 60+) is being squeezed from both ends, and the AP aging is the downstream consequence of the AR problem. Conversely, a business with healthy collections but a high AP 60+ share is choosing to stretch payables, which is a different conversation. Looking at AP aging in isolation can misdiagnose the cause; pairing it with AR aging and DSO shows whether the late payments are a symptom of slow collections or a deliberate cash strategy. The working-capital cycle is the frame, and this card is one of its two halves.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

AP aging is native to NetSuite, so this card triangulates directly against the standard payables reports:

> **Reports → Vendors/Payables → A/P Aging** (the canonical aging report; the 61-90 and 91+ columns are the card's numerator)
> **Reports → Vendors/Payables → A/P Aging Detail** (bill-level detail behind each aging bucket, for drilling into specific overdue bills)
> **Reports → Vendors/Payables → A/P Register** (open payables with dates, useful for confirming what counts as open)
> **Saved search**: a Transaction saved search on open vendor bills, with an age formula bucketing days outstanding, grouped by subsidiary and vendor; this reproduces the card's split
> **SuiteAnalytics workbook**: an AP workbook with an aging dimension sliced by subsidiary gives the per-subsidiary 60+ share the card aggregates

The headline difference is that NetSuite's A/P Aging report shows the buckets as balances and requires you to compute the 60+ share yourself and to remember last period's figure. The card computes the percentage, presents it as a gauge, and shows the prior-period direction automatically.

**Why our number may legitimately differ from NetSuite's A/P Aging report:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aging basis (bill date vs due date)** | Either | The card ages from the configured basis; an A/P Aging report set to a different basis will bucket the same bill differently, especially for non-standard terms. |
| **As-of date** | Either | A real-time card and a report run as of a different date will disagree by any bills that aged across the boundary or were paid in between. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Either | The card respects the dashboard filter; a report run at the parent without scoping includes or excludes subs differently. |
| **Partial payments** | Either | A partially paid bill contributes only its remaining balance; reports that age the original amount rather than the open balance will differ. |
| **Credits and debit memos** | Either | Vendor credits applied against bills reduce the open balance; whether a report nets them affects the percentage. |
| **FX translation timing** | Either | Foreign-currency bills translated at current vs period-end FX shift the reporting-currency balances and therefore the share. |
| **Disputed or on-hold bills** | Either | Bills flagged in dispute are still open and still age; some manual reports exclude them, which changes the denominator and numerator. |
| **Prepayments and deposits** | Either | Vendor prepayments sitting as debit balances can offset payables; treatment differs between the card and ad-hoc reports. |

There is no cross-connector reconciliation for this card. AP aging is a NetSuite payables concept with no counterpart in commerce or ad platforms, so the only valid comparison is against NetSuite's own A/P aging reports and saved searches.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**What does the 60+ share actually measure?**
It is the portion of everything you currently owe vendors that has been unpaid for 60 days or more, divided by your total open payables, shown as a percentage. The numerator rolls together the 61-90 and 91+ aging buckets; the denominator is your whole open AP balance.

**Why 60 days and not 30?**
Because most vendors offer roughly 30-day net terms, so a bill in the 31-60 bucket is often still within or barely past terms. The 60+ threshold isolates payables that are genuinely overdue for the typical supplier, which is the population that strains relationships and risks late fees or credit holds.

**Is a rising 60+ share always a cash problem?**
No. There are three common causes. Deliberate, negotiated stretching of cooperative suppliers is legitimate working-capital management. A process failure (bills stuck in approval, an unresolved dispute, a statement mismatch) is a fixable operational issue. A genuine cash shortfall is the dangerous one. The card shows the share; the composition tells you which story you are in. Pair with the pending-approvals card to separate stuck process from no cash.

**Why is the trend as important as the level?**
Because a comfortable 20% that was 14% last period is heading the wrong way, and catching that early lets you fix the cause while it is small. The gauge shows the current level; the prior-period comparison shows direction. Waiting for the absolute level to breach the alert threshold means reacting to a problem that has already strained suppliers.

**How does OneWorld change the reading?**
Each subsidiary has its own AP, its own team, and often its own cash position, so a consolidated percentage can hide a single subsidiary in distress. An aggregate 20% can mask a UK sub at 38% behind a US parent at 12%. Always drill the gauge by subsidiary before concluding the group is healthy, and give per-subsidiary readings to the AP managers who actually control payment timing.

**Multi-currency and FX, how do they affect it?**
Foreign-currency vendor bills are translated to the reporting currency for the aging calculation, so a moving exchange rate can shift the reporting-currency balances and nudge the percentage even when nothing operational changed. For period-over-period comparisons, a consistent FX cadence keeps the trend honest. The aging itself (days outstanding) is unaffected by FX; only the translated balances move.

**B2B vs DTC, does the card read differently?**
The mechanism is identical, but the context differs. B2B and wholesale operations typically carry larger, more concentrated payables to a smaller set of strategic suppliers, so a high 60+ share against a key supplier is a sharper supply risk. DTC brands often have more fragmented payables (many small marketing, fulfilment, and SaaS vendors), so the same percentage spreads across less critical relationships. Read the share alongside which vendors sit in the 60+ bucket, not just the headline number.

**Should the alert really be 30%?**
The 30% default is a generic ceiling, not a universal rule. Your right threshold depends on your payment-terms profile and vendor mix. A business that has negotiated extended terms with its major suppliers might legitimately run higher; a business on strict 30-day terms across the board should alarm sooner. Tune the threshold per workspace so it reflects your actual terms.

**What is the difference between aging from bill date and from due date?**
Aging from bill date counts days since the invoice was dated; aging from due date counts days past when payment was actually owed. For standard net-30 terms the two differ by 30 days, which can move a bill between buckets. The card uses the configured basis; make sure it matches how your finance team and your A/P Aging report are set up, or the numbers will look inconsistent.

**How do partial payments and credits affect it?**
A partially paid bill contributes only its remaining open balance to the aging, and applied vendor credits reduce the open balance. So a bill that was large but is mostly paid contributes little to the 60+ share. Reports that age the original bill amount rather than the open balance will read higher than the card, which uses the open balance.

**Does this card interact with period close?**
It reflects the current open payables, so it reads at any time, but the numbers are most reliable once the period's vendor bills are fully entered and reconciled. During an open period, bills still being entered can shift the denominator. Pair with the period-close cards if you are using the AP aging share for board reporting, so you know whether the underlying AP is final.

**How does this compare to other ERPs?**
Every accounting platform produces an AP aging, but the bucketing basis, the treatment of credits and prepayments, and the FX handling differ. NetSuite's A/P Aging is the native source this card reads. Oracle and SAP produce comparable aging analyses with their own configuration nuances. The card normalises NetSuite's aging into a single 60+ percentage with a trend, which reads the same regardless of how the underlying report is configured, as long as the aging basis is set consistently.
