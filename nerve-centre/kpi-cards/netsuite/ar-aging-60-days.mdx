> Share of receivables aged 60 or more days; the single cleanest collections-health gauge.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> The percentage of your open accounts receivable that has been outstanding for 60 days or more, measured against the total open AR balance. It is the cleanest single number for collections health because it isolates the receivables that have genuinely slipped, stripping out the recent invoices that are simply not due yet. When this gauge climbs, cash you have already earned is sitting in customer hands instead of your bank.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | Open AR balance aged 60 or more days as a share of total open AR. Aging is measured from invoice date or due date depending on the workspace aging basis. |
| **Currency** | Reporting currency (consolidated). Per-subsidiary views available in local currency. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Respects dashboard filter. OneWorld accounts roll up across subs; single-sub accounts show the one entity. |
| **Time window** | `30D vsP` (current reading versus prior 30-day period for trend) |
| **Alert trigger** | Greater than 15% of total open AR aged 60 or more days |
| **Roles** | owner, finance |
| **Aging basis** | Invoice date or due date, per workspace policy. Due-date basis is stricter and recommended for terms-based B2B. |
| **Excludes** | Credit memos in some configurations; fully paid invoices; draft and unapproved invoices. |
{/* /HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}

## Calculation

The card sums the open balance on every receivable transaction whose age exceeds the 60-day threshold, then divides that figure by the total open AR balance for the same scope. Age is computed from either the invoice date or the due date, whichever the workspace has chosen as its aging basis, and the result is expressed as a percentage. The numerator captures the truly delinquent slice; the denominator captures everything still owed, including current and not-yet-due invoices. Because it is a ratio, the gauge is insensitive to the absolute size of your AR book, which makes it comparable across a growing business or against a peer of a different size.

The reading respects the dashboard subsidiary filter and the reporting currency. On a OneWorld account, per-subsidiary balances are translated to the reporting currency before the ratio is taken, so a single weakening currency can nudge the consolidated number even when no operational change occurred. The card does not attempt to predict whether a given invoice will eventually be paid; it simply states how much of what is owed has aged past the threshold today. The worked example below shows a typical reading and how to interpret a move.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A US home-and-garden brand on NetSuite OneWorld selling both DTC through Shopify and wholesale into regional garden centres. Three subsidiaries: a US parent (USD), a UK sub (GBP), and an EU sub (EUR). Annual revenue roughly $42M, of which about 60% is wholesale on net-30 and net-60 terms. Reporting currency USD. The 30-day window runs 14 Mar 26 to 12 Apr 26, compared against the prior period 12 Feb 26 to 13 Mar 26.

| Aging bucket | Open AR (USD) | Share of total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current (not yet due) | $3,180,000 | 44.4% |
| 1-30 days | $1,640,000 | 22.9% |
| 31-60 days | $1,020,000 | 14.2% |
| 61-90 days | $760,000 | 10.6% |
| 91+ days | $560,000 | 7.8% |
| **Total open AR** | **$7,160,000** | **100%** |
| **Aged 60+ days (this card)** | **$1,320,000** | **18.4%** |
| Prior period 60+ share | | 13.1% |

Five things to notice:

1. **At 18.4% the gauge is over the 15% alert threshold, and the move from 13.1% in the prior period is the story, not the absolute level.** A single high reading can be a snapshot quirk: one large customer who always pays on day 65, a seasonal billing bulge, or a quarter-end push that loaded invoices early. The 5.3 point jump in 30 days is what should pull a Controller out of their chair, because it means receivables are aging faster than they are being collected, and that pattern compounds. The reason the trend matters more than the level is that healthy businesses run at a stable 60+ share that reflects their customer mix and terms; a rising share means the collections engine is falling behind inflow, and every period it stays elevated, more current invoices roll into the aged bucket.

2. **The $1.32M sitting in the 60+ bucket is cash you have already earned and already paid COGS on, now financing your customers interest-free.** This is the visceral framing finance teams respond to. You bought the inventory, you shipped it, you booked the revenue and the cost, and the only thing standing between you and the cash is a customer who has not paid. At a 12% cost of capital, carrying $1.32M of aged receivables for an extra 60 days costs roughly $26K in financing alone, before you even consider the rising probability of write-off as invoices age. The card turns an abstract aging report into a financing-cost conversation that a board understands immediately.

3. **The 91+ bucket at $560K is the dangerous tail, because collectability falls steeply with age.** Industry collection-probability curves typically show roughly 95% recovery on invoices under 30 days, dropping toward 70-75% at 90 days and below 50% beyond 120 days for many B2B portfolios. The $560K in the 91+ bucket is therefore not worth $560K on a risk-adjusted basis; a Controller should mentally reserve a meaningful slice of it. The card flags the share; the next move is to drill into [A/R Aging Detail](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/a-r-aging-detail) to see which customers populate the tail, because aged AR is almost always concentrated in a handful of names rather than spread evenly.

4. **A rising 60+ share is the leading indicator that [DSO](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/days-sales-outstanding) is about to climb and that more orders are heading for credit hold.** These three cards form a chain: aged AR rises first, DSO follows as the average collection period lengthens, and then NetSuite's credit-limit logic starts flipping customers onto credit hold as their open balances breach limits, which in turn blocks new sales orders. Catching the move on this gauge gives the finance team a head start of one to two weeks over the DSO reading and several weeks over the credit-hold disruption. That lead time is the whole point of a hero gauge: it is early enough to act before the problem reaches the order book.

5. **On a OneWorld account the consolidated 18.4% can hide a single problem subsidiary.** Suppose the US parent runs a healthy 11% and the UK sub runs 34% because one large garden-centre chain has slipped two months behind. The consolidated number averages to a moderate-looking 18.4%, but the actionable truth lives in the UK sub. Per-subsidiary views are essential here: the consolidated gauge tells you something is wrong, and the per-sub breakdown tells you where. The most useful operational pattern is to watch the consolidated number for the alert and immediately decompose by subsidiary when it trips, rather than reading the consolidated number as if it were uniform across the group.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

NetSuite produces aging natively, so this card has a clean reconciliation path. The card mirrors the standard A/R Aging report logic but applies a fixed 60-day cut and expresses the result as a single ratio. The closest native views to triangulate against:

> **Reports → Customers/Receivables → A/R Aging Summary** (the headline buckets; sum the 61-90 and 91+ columns, divide by total)
> **Reports → Customers/Receivables → A/R Aging Detail** (invoice-level, the source of truth for which transactions sit in each bucket)
> **Reports → Financial → Balance Sheet** (the Accounts Receivable line should tie to the total open AR denominator, subject to in-transit timing)
> **Saved search**: a Transaction saved search filtered to open invoices with an age formula `daysOpen` bucketed at 60, summing amount remaining
> **Reports → Customers/Receivables → A/R Aging by Subsidiary** (for OneWorld; per-sub buckets, essential when one sub drives the consolidated number)

The single most common reason a manual A/R Aging report and this card disagree is the aging basis. NetSuite lets you age by transaction date or by due date, and the two produce materially different bucket boundaries for any business that grants payment terms. A net-60 invoice aged by invoice date enters the 60+ bucket the moment it is issued for the buyer's clock; aged by due date it only enters when it is genuinely overdue. Confirm which basis your workspace and your NetSuite report each use before concluding the numbers are wrong.

**Why our number may legitimately differ from the native A/R Aging report:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aging basis (invoice date vs due date)** | Either | The largest single cause of divergence. Due-date basis pushes invoices into the aged bucket later; invoice-date basis pushes them in sooner. Match the basis on both sides. |
| **As-of date / refresh cadence** | Either | The native report is run as-of a chosen date; the card refreshes on its own cadence. A few days of drift between the two reshuffles bucket membership at the margins. |
| **Credit memo treatment** | Card may read higher or lower | If the workspace nets open credit memos against aged invoices and the native report does not (or vice versa), the denominator and numerator shift. |
| **Unapplied payments and deposits** | Either | Cash received but not yet applied to a specific invoice can leave the invoice showing as fully open and aged on one side but partly settled on the other. |
| **FX translation** | Small | Per-sub balances translated to reporting currency at the card's FX cadence; the native report may use a different as-of rate. |
| **Inter-co receivables** | Card may read lower | The consolidated card uses post-elimination values; per-sub reports include inter-co AR that consolidation removes. |
| **Statistical and zero-balance accounts** | Small | Some saved searches include statistical accounts or zero-balance customers that the standard report excludes, nudging the denominator. |
| **Disputed or on-hold invoices** | Either | Invoices flagged in dispute may be excluded from one view and included in the other depending on configuration. |

The headline difference to expect is small when both sides use the same aging basis and as-of date. If the gap is larger than a point or two, the basis or the credit-memo treatment is almost always the cause.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**Why 60 days and not 90?**
60 days is the inflection point where a receivable stops looking like normal terms friction and starts looking like a collections problem. Most B2B terms run net-30 to net-60, so an invoice past 60 days is overdue under almost any terms structure, while a 90-day cut would let genuinely late net-60 invoices hide in the "current-ish" band for a full extra month. 60 days catches the slip early enough to act, which is the entire value of a hero gauge. If your portfolio runs predominantly net-60 or net-90 terms, ask your workspace admin whether a due-date aging basis is the better fit, because that keeps the threshold meaningful regardless of how generous your terms are.

**How does OneWorld change the reading?**
The formula is identical; OneWorld simply scopes it across subsidiaries and translates each sub's balance to the reporting currency before taking the ratio. The risk is that the consolidated number averages out a single problem sub. Always decompose by subsidiary when the alert trips. A consolidated 18% that is actually 11% in the US and 34% in the UK is a UK collections problem, not a group-wide one, and treating it as group-wide wastes effort across healthy subs.

**Does multi-currency distort the percentage?**
The ratio itself is currency-neutral within a single sub because numerator and denominator are in the same currency. Distortion only enters at the consolidated level, where a weakening sub currency shrinks both that sub's aged balance and its total in reporting-currency terms proportionally, so the per-sub ratio is preserved but its weight in the consolidated average shifts. For period-over-period comparisons, lock the FX cadence so a currency move does not masquerade as a collections change.

**B2B versus DTC, do I read this differently?**
Yes, fundamentally. DTC orders are paid at checkout, so a pure DTC business has almost no AR and this card is near zero by definition; a non-trivial reading on a DTC-only book usually means chargebacks, payment-gateway holds, or marketplace payout lags rather than classic credit aging. B2B is where this card earns its keep, because B2B sells on terms and the aged bucket is real credit risk. A hybrid business should expect the aged share to track the wholesale mix: the more of revenue that is B2B-on-terms, the more meaningful this gauge.

**Single-subsidiary versus OneWorld behaviour?**
Identical calculation. A single-sub account shows one entity in its functional currency with no consolidation or FX layer, which makes reconciliation against the native report cleaner. OneWorld adds the translation and elimination layers described above. The interpretation discipline (watch the trend, decompose by sub) applies to OneWorld; single-sub accounts skip the decomposition step.

**Does period close affect this card?**
Indirectly. The card reads live open AR, so it does not wait for the period to close. However, late cash application and adjusting entries posted during close can move invoices in and out of the aged bucket, so the reading taken mid-close can differ from the one taken after the period is locked. For a clean board number, take the reading after the relevant period close; for an operational early warning, the live reading is the right one.

**Why might the card read higher than my collections team expects?**
Two usual suspects. First, a slow [Cash Application Rate](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/cash-application-rate): if customers have paid but the cash has not been applied to their invoices yet, those invoices still show as open and aged. The money is in the bank but the card cannot see it as settled. Second, disputed invoices that the team has mentally written off but that remain open in NetSuite. Both inflate the numerator without reflecting genuine new risk.

**Does it include credit memos and deposits?**
Configurable. Some workspaces net open credit memos and customer deposits against aged invoices before computing the share, which lowers the numerator; others count gross open invoices only. Check your workspace setting and match it to whatever your native A/R Aging report does before reconciling. A mismatch here is one of the most common sources of a small persistent gap between the card and the report.

**How does this relate to credit hold?**
Closely. As a customer's invoices age past 60 days, their open balance grows, which pushes their [Customer Credit Utilisation](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/customer-credit-utilisation) toward their limit, which eventually trips NetSuite's credit-hold logic and blocks their new orders. A rising 60+ share is therefore an early warning that more orders are about to be blocked. Watching this gauge gives sales and finance time to intervene with the customer before the order book is disrupted.

**What is a healthy reading?**
It depends on your terms and customer mix, but for a typical mid-market B2B book a 60+ share in the 5-12% band is normal, 12-15% is worth watching, and above 15% (the alert) signals the collections engine is falling behind. A DTC-heavy book should sit near zero. The right benchmark is your own stable baseline; a number that is fine for one business is alarming for another, so weight the trend over the absolute level.

**Can it be gamed by writing off bad debt?**
Yes, mechanically. Writing off an aged invoice removes it from open AR and instantly lowers the 60+ share, which can make a deteriorating book look like it improved. This is legitimate accounting when the debt is genuinely uncollectable, but as a metric move it should be read alongside [Credit Memos Value](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/ns-credit-memos-value) and write-off activity so a clean-up is not mistaken for a collections win.

**NetSuite versus other ERPs on aging?**
NetSuite's native aging is solid and this card mirrors it, so reconciliation is straightforward once the aging basis matches. Oracle ERP Cloud and SAP S/4HANA both produce comparable aging buckets through their AR modules, though the configuration of due-date versus document-date aging and the handling of partial payments differs in the details. The value Vortex IQ adds is not a better aging report; it is the live gauge plus the trend alert plus the join to credit utilisation and the order book, which turns a static month-end report into a continuous early-warning signal.
