> Accounting Period Close Status: the live state of the current or most-recent accounting period - open, in progress, or locked - and how many days that sits against the close target.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> Where your books stand right now: is the current or most-recent period still open, partway through close, or fully locked, and how does that compare to when it should have closed. It is the single status read a finance lead checks before relying on any other number, because an open period means the books are still moving and every downstream figure is provisional. Because the state of the close gates the trustworthiness of the entire reporting stack, this is a Hero card.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | The close state of the current or most-recent accounting period, expressed as open, in progress (partially locked), or locked, with the days versus the target close date. On OneWorld, one state per subsidiary, since each sub closes independently. |
| **States** | Open (still posting), in progress (some transaction types or steps locked, others open), and locked (period closed, numbers final). |
| **Tax treatment** | n/a directly, but an unlocked period means tax provisions and filings based on it are not yet final. |
| **Currency** | Reporting currency for any value context; the status itself is currency-agnostic. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Respects the dashboard filter. On OneWorld, each subsidiary locks separately, so a single calendar period can be locked for one sub and open for another. |
| **Time window** | `RT` (real-time; reflects the current open/locked state of the period). |
| **Alert trigger** | Fires when the period is `overdue greater than 5 days` against its close target while still unlocked. Tune to your close-calendar SLA. |

## Calculation

The card reads the lock state of the current or most-recent accounting period and compares it against the period's target close date from your close calendar. It resolves the state into three readings: open (the period is still accepting postings), in progress (some transaction types or steps are locked while others remain open, a partial close), and locked (the period is closed and its numbers are final). Alongside the state it shows how many days the period sits relative to its target, so a period that is locked early, on time, or overdue is immediately legible.

On OneWorld the card reports per subsidiary, because each subsidiary locks its own period independently and a single calendar month can be locked for some subs and open for others. The default alert fires once a period is more than five days past its target while still unlocked, which gives a normal close a few days of grace before it is treated as slipping. The card does not diagnose why a period is open; it reports the state and the timing so the controller knows at a glance whether the books are safe to rely on, and the companion overdue-list card surfaces the specific periods that have slipped.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A multi-brand commerce group on NetSuite OneWorld. Four subsidiaries: a US parent (USD), a UK sub (GBP), an EU sub (EUR), and an APAC sub (AUD). Annual revenue roughly $130M, with a ten-day consolidation close calendar. Snapshot taken 16 Jun 26, when the May period should be locked across the group. Reporting context USD. These figures are illustrative.

| Period | Subsidiary | Target close date | Status | Days vs target |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| May 26 | US parent | 09 Jun 26 | Locked | -2 (closed early) |
| May 26 | UK sub | 09 Jun 26 | Locked | 0 (on time) |
| May 26 | EU sub | 09 Jun 26 | In progress (AR open) | +7 (overdue) |
| May 26 | APAC sub | 09 Jun 26 | Open | +7 (overdue) |
| **Group close** | | | **Partially closed** | **2 subs overdue** |

Five things to notice:

1. **The group-level answer to "is May closed" is genuinely "partly", and that is the whole reason the card reports per subsidiary instead of a single flag.** Two subs are locked and two are not, so a single consolidated yes/no would either lie (saying "closed" when two subs are open) or be uselessly alarming (saying "open" when half the group is done). By showing four distinct states, the card turns an ambiguous group status into a precise picture: the US and UK are safe to report, the EU is nearly there with AR still open, and the APAC sub has not meaningfully started. A controller reading this in ten seconds knows exactly which two teams need help and which two are clear.

2. **"In progress" is a real and dangerous state, not a rounding of "almost closed".** The EU sub has locked most transaction types but left AR open, perhaps because a handful of customer payments are still being applied. At a glance the EU close looks essentially done, but because AR is open, May revenue and receivables for the EU sub can still change. The card deliberately shows this as in progress rather than locked, which is the conservative and correct reading: until every stream that affects the financials is locked, the period's numbers are not final and should not be reported as such. This distinction stops the common mistake of treating a partial close as a finished one.

3. **The days-versus-target column is what converts a status into an action, and the sign matters.** The US parent closed two days early (a healthy sign of a well-run close), the UK closed exactly on time, and the EU and APAC are both seven days over. The negative number is not a problem to chase; the positive ones are. By putting the timing next to the state, the card lets the controller sort attention by urgency: the seven-day-overdue APAC open period is the priority, the EU in-progress period is the second, and the two locked subs need no attention at all. A status without timing tells you the state; the timing tells you whether to act.

4. **Because this is the gate on every other number, the close status is read first and the rest of the dashboard second.** If the board reviews May figures on 16 Jun and this card shows two subs still open, then May revenue by subsidiary, gross margin, AR aging, cash collected, and intercompany balances for those subs are all provisional and can move after the meeting. The honest workflow is to check this card before quoting any May number for the EU or APAC subs, and to caveat those numbers as draft. This is exactly why the card is Hero-class: it is not just another metric, it is the trust indicator that tells you how much weight every other metric can bear.

5. **The five-day grace keeps the alert credible on a ten-day close calendar.** Closes routinely run a little over target for benign reasons: a late bank feed, a delayed vendor bill, a public holiday in one region. If the card alarmed on the first day a period was open past target, the team would learn to ignore it. By only flagging overdue beyond five days, the card separates a normal close that ran slightly long from one that is genuinely stuck. On this snapshot, both the EU and APAC subs are seven days over, comfortably past the grace, so both legitimately trip the alert. The grace is tunable per workspace, because a three-day DTC close wants a tighter window than this group's ten-day consolidation.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

Period close is native to NetSuite via the period-management tooling and the close checklist, so this card triangulates directly against the system's own close state:

> **Setup → Accounting → Manage Accounting Periods** (the master list of periods with lock status per subsidiary and transaction type, the source of this card's state)
> **Period Close Checklist** (the step-by-step close workflow; an incomplete checklist is why a period reads "in progress" rather than "locked")
> **Reports → Financial → Period End Financials** (these are provisional until the period locks; running them tests whether numbers are still moving)
> **Saved search**: an accounting-period search returning each period with its closed flag and target close date, grouped by subsidiary, is essentially the state this card resolves
> **SuiteAnalytics workbook**: an Accounting Periods workbook joined to per-subsidiary lock status, sliced by days-versus-target, reproduces the card's per-sub status view

The headline difference is that NetSuite's Manage Accounting Periods page shows the lock state, but it does not, on its own, compute "days versus target" or alert you when an unlocked period crosses the threshold. The card adds the timing comparison and the alerting layer on top of NetSuite's raw lock status.

**Why our status may legitimately differ from a glance at NetSuite's period list:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Partial locks** | Card may read more cautiously | The card reports "in progress" when any transaction type is open; a glance at the period list might read it as essentially closed. |
| **Subsidiary breakout** | Card may show more states | The card reports per subsidiary; a consolidated view shows one period that may be locked for some subs and open for others. |
| **Target-date definition** | Either | The card uses the workspace-configured target close date and grace; if NetSuite's close-calendar dates differ from the workspace setting, the days-versus-target reading can differ. |
| **Grace window** | Card may alert later | The default only alerts beyond five days overdue; a raw "is it closed" view treats day one of overdue the same as day ten. |
| **Adjustment periods** | Either | Year-end adjustment periods have their own close timing and may be shown or excluded depending on configuration. |
| **Reopened periods** | Card may flip back to open | A period reopened to post a late adjustment becomes open again; the card reflects that while a static report may still show the original close. |
| **Multi-book accounting** | Either | A period can be locked in the primary book but open in a secondary book; the card follows the configured book. |

There is no cross-connector reconciliation for this card. Period close is a NetSuite GL governance concept with no equivalent in commerce or ad platforms, so the only valid comparison is against NetSuite's own period-management tooling. The card's job is to make that native state legible and timely, not to compare it against another system.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**What is the difference between open, in progress, and locked?**
Open means the period is still accepting postings and its numbers can change freely. In progress means some transaction types or close steps are locked while others remain open, a partial close, so the period is not yet final. Locked means the period is closed and its numbers are final. The card reports the state plus days versus target so you know not just where the close stands but whether it is early, on time, or slipping.

**Why does a single month show several statuses?**
On OneWorld, each subsidiary closes its own period independently, so the same calendar month can be locked for some subsidiaries and open for others. The card reports one status per subsidiary, which is what makes the group picture honest and the work assignable to a specific team rather than hidden behind a single consolidated flag.

**Why is "in progress" treated so seriously?**
Because a partially locked period still has open streams whose numbers can change. If AR is open, revenue and receivables can move; if AP is open, expenses can move. The period "looks closed" but is not final. The card flags in progress as distinct from locked precisely so a partial close is not mistaken for a finished one, which is one of the most common reporting errors.

**Why is this a Hero card?**
Because the close status gates the trustworthiness of every other financial number. An open or in-progress period means revenue, margin, AR, cash, and intercompany figures are all provisional. Reporting provisional numbers to a board, a lender, or an auditor and then having them move erodes trust in the whole reporting stack. Knowing the close state first is what lets you trust everything else, so it is high-value enough to be Hero-class.

**How do I set the right target date and grace window?**
The target date comes from your close calendar and should reflect your real close SLA. The grace window (default five days) should match how much slack a normal close legitimately needs. A fast DTC business closing in three days wants a tight grace; a complex OneWorld group with a ten-day consolidation wants more. Calibrate so the card only alerts on genuine slips, not routine overruns.

**Multi-currency and FX, do they affect the status?**
The status itself is about lock state and dates, not currency, so FX does not move it. FX matters only for any value context shown per row, which uses the reporting currency. Note that an open period means the period's FX revaluation has not been finalised, which is one more reason its numbers are provisional until it locks.

**B2B versus DTC, does the reading differ?**
The mechanism is identical, but multi-entity B2B groups generally have longer, more complex closes (intercompany eliminations, more reconciliations, more subsidiaries) and so a longer legitimate close calendar. DTC single-entity businesses often close in a few days. Set the target and grace to your own model so the status and its timing are meaningful rather than generic.

**What usually keeps a period from locking?**
The common blockers are unreconciled accounts (especially bank and clearing accounts), transactions pending approval, errored or unbalanced transactions, failed integration syncs from commerce or banking feeds, and missing accruals. This card tells you the period is not locked; the sibling cards (open reconciliation items, pending approvals, transaction imbalances) help you find the specific blocker holding it open.

**Can a period be reopened, and does the status reflect that?**
Yes. Finance sometimes reopens a closed period to post a late adjustment. While reopened, the period is open again and the card reflects that, flipping the status back from locked. This is intentional: a reopened period's numbers are once again provisional until it relocks, and pretending otherwise would be misleading.

**Does a SuiteScript or integration failure affect the close status?**
Indirectly and often. If a scheduled SuiteScript that posts daily sales summaries or applies payments fails, the affected accounts will not reconcile and the period cannot lock, so the status stays open. The card shows the symptom (period still open); pair it with the SuiteScript error and reconciliation cards to connect the technical failure to the delayed close.

**How does this compare to period control in other ERPs?**
Every serious ERP enforces period close, but the granularity differs. NetSuite locks per subsidiary and per transaction type, which is why the card reports at that grain. Oracle and SAP have their own posting-period and period-control mechanisms with comparable concepts under different names. The card normalises NetSuite's lock model into a clear open/in-progress/locked status with days-versus-target, so the reading is consistent regardless of how the underlying ERP labels its states.

**Does the card handle year-end and adjustment periods?**
Yes, with a caveat. Year-end and adjustment periods have their own close timing and target dates that can differ from regular monthly periods. As long as those dates are configured in the close calendar, the card reports their status on the same basis. If an adjustment period is configured without a target date, its days-versus-target may not be evaluated; confirm your close-calendar setup includes target dates for those periods.
