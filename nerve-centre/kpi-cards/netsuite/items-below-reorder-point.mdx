> Items Below Reorder Point: a live count of inventory items whose available quantity has dropped under their configured NetSuite reorder point, the replenishment work queue.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> How many inventory items have fallen below the reorder point you set in NetSuite, meaning they are due to be reordered now to avoid running out. It is the daily replenishment work queue for the buying and operations teams: each item below its reorder point is a purchase order that should be raised before the item stocks out. A rising count signals demand running ahead of supply, lengthening lead times, or reorder points that have drifted out of date.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | Inventory items whose available quantity has fallen below their configured reorder point. One per item (and per location where reorder points are set per location). |
| **What "available" means** | The quantity free to commit to new demand, which typically nets committed and on-order quantities against on-hand, not raw on-hand alone. This is why an item with stock on the shelf can still be below reorder if most of it is already committed. |
| **Tax treatment** | n/a, this is a count of items, not a value. The replenishment cost is read from the purchase side once orders are raised. |
| **Currency** | Currency-agnostic for the count; replenishment cost when quantified uses the item's purchase currency and the reporting currency for totals. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Respects the dashboard filter. On OneWorld, items and reorder points can be defined per subsidiary and per location, so the count is attributable to a sub and a warehouse. |
| **Time window** | `RT` (real-time; the count reflects current available quantity against current reorder points). |
| **Alert trigger** | Fires when there are `greater than 0` items below reorder point, so the queue is never silently non-empty. Tune if a small standing queue is normal for you. |
| **Roles** | owner, operations |
| **Chart type** | Single KPI count with a non-zero alert. |
| **What it excludes** | Items with no reorder point set (they cannot be below a threshold that does not exist), discontinued items where replenishment is intentionally off, and non-inventory item types. |
{/* /HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}

## Calculation

The card counts inventory items whose available quantity has fallen below the reorder point configured for them in NetSuite. The reorder point is the level at which an item should be reordered to avoid stocking out before replenishment arrives; it is usually set to cover expected demand over the supplier lead time plus a safety buffer. Available quantity is the figure that can still be committed to new demand, which generally nets what is already committed and what is already on order against the physical on-hand, so the card reflects true replenishment urgency rather than just shelf count.

Because reorder points and quantities can be defined per location, the count is typically evaluated per item per location and then totalled. The card does not raise purchase orders or judge whether the reorder points themselves are correct; it reports which items have crossed their threshold so the buying team can act. A persistently empty queue can mean reorder points are healthy, or that they are set too low and the business is stocking out before items ever register as below reorder. A constantly large queue can mean genuine demand pressure, or reorder points that have drifted above realistic levels. The count is the signal; interpreting it requires the lead-time and demand context the operations team holds.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A DTC and wholesale outdoor-gear brand on NetSuite OneWorld. Two subsidiaries: a US parent (USD) with two warehouses (East and West), and a UK sub (GBP) with one warehouse. Roughly 4,200 active SKUs. Annual revenue around $70M. Snapshot 18 Jun 26, mid-season. Reporting context USD. These figures are illustrative.

| Location | Items below reorder point | Of which already on a PO | Truly exposed (no PO) | Typical driver |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| US East warehouse | 61 | 44 | 17 | Steady demand, lead times stretching |
| US West warehouse | 38 | 30 | 8 | Recent transfer out depleted stock |
| UK warehouse | 23 | 12 | 11 | Longer import lead times, smaller buffer |
| **Total** | **122** | **86** | **36** | |

Five things to notice:

1. **The headline of 122 below reorder point overstates the real risk, because 86 of those already have a purchase order in flight, and the number that should keep the buyer awake is 36.** An item below its reorder point with a PO already raised is the system working exactly as intended: the threshold triggered, someone ordered, and replenishment is on its way. The genuinely exposed items are the 36 below reorder with no PO, because those are the ones that will stock out if nobody acts. Reading the raw 122 leads to either panic or fatigue; reading the 36 directs the buying team at the precise set of items that need a purchase order today. This is the single most important refinement when interpreting this card: separate "below reorder, covered" from "below reorder, uncovered".

2. **Available quantity, not on-hand, is what trips the threshold, which is why an item can show here with stock visibly on the shelf.** Several of the US East items still have physical units in the warehouse, but most of that stock is already committed to open sales orders, so the available quantity, what can still be promised to new demand, is below the reorder point. If the card used raw on-hand it would miss these and the items would stock out for new orders while appearing "in stock". Using available quantity makes the card a true forward-looking replenishment signal rather than a backward-looking shelf count. The practical consequence is that you cannot dismiss an item on this card by walking to the shelf and seeing boxes; you have to check how much of that stock is already spoken for.

3. **A rising count is a leading indicator with three common causes, and telling them apart is the operations team's job.** First, demand genuinely accelerating (a product taking off, a season peaking) pulls available quantity down faster than reorder points anticipated. Second, supplier lead times stretching means the reorder point, which was sized for a shorter lead time, is now too low, so items dip below it later than they should and the buffer is thin. Third, reorder points drifting out of date, never updated after demand shifted, can make the count rise or fall for reasons that have nothing to do with current reality. On this snapshot the US East note is "lead times stretching", which is the second cause and the dangerous one, because it means the reorder points themselves need raising, not just more POs.

4. **The UK warehouse has the worst exposure ratio, 11 uncovered out of 23, and that pattern points at a structural buffer problem, not a one-off.** With nearly half its below-reorder items uncovered and a driver of "longer import lead times, smaller buffer", the UK sub is systematically under-buffered for the lead times it actually faces. A US warehouse covering 44 of 61 is reacting well; the UK covering only 12 of 23 is reacting poorly, and the cause is likely that UK reorder points were set assuming domestic-style lead times when the reality is longer international supply. This is exactly the kind of cross-location comparison the card enables: the absolute count per location matters less than the covered-versus-uncovered ratio, which reveals which warehouse's replenishment discipline or reorder-point configuration is weakest.

5. **A recent inter-warehouse transfer explains the US West count, which is a reminder that this card reacts to internal movement, not just sales.** The West warehouse's items dipped below reorder because stock was transferred out to cover demand elsewhere, not because of a sales surge. The card correctly flags them, because available quantity genuinely fell, but the right action may be to replenish from the supplier, to transfer stock back, or to rebalance the transfer policy, rather than simply raising a PO. On OneWorld and multi-location accounts, transfers, allocations, and location-level reorder points all feed this card, so a spike at one location should always prompt the question "did this come from sales, from a transfer, or from a reorder-point change", because the answer changes the fix.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

Reorder points and available quantity are native to NetSuite inventory, so this card triangulates against the item records and the standard replenishment views:

> **Lists → Accounting → Items** (each item record carries its reorder point, preferred stock level, and per-location settings)
> **Transactions → Inventory → Order Items** or the Replenishment / Reorder reports (NetSuite's native view of what is due to be reordered)
> **Reports → Inventory → Inventory Reorder** or **Items Below Reorder Point** style saved reports (the closest native equivalent to this card)
> **Saved search**: an Item saved search comparing `available quantity` against `reorder point` per location, filtered to where available is below reorder, is essentially the population the card counts
> **SuiteAnalytics workbook**: an Items workbook joined to location-level inventory, sliced by available-versus-reorder, reproduces the per-location below-reorder count

The headline difference is that NetSuite's reorder reports show which items are due to be reordered, but the exact definition of "due" depends on whether demand planning, lead times, and on-order quantities are factored in. This card reports the simpler, transparent condition, available quantity below the configured reorder point, and adds the live count and the non-zero alert on top of NetSuite's native inventory state.

**Why our count may legitimately differ from a NetSuite reorder report:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Definition of available** | Either | The card nets committed and on-order against on-hand; a report using raw on-hand, or a demand-planning-adjusted figure, will count differently. |
| **On-order handling** | Either | If an item is below reorder but already on a PO, NetSuite's demand-planning reorder report may exclude it as "handled"; the card counts the condition regardless, and shows covered versus uncovered separately. |
| **Per-location reorder points** | Either | Reorder points set per location mean an item can be below reorder at one warehouse and fine at another; a company-level view blends these. |
| **Items with no reorder point** | Card shows fewer | Items without a configured reorder point cannot be below it; a planner manually judging stock might flag them anyway. |
| **Discontinued or inactive items** | Card shows fewer | Items intentionally not replenished are excluded; a raw available-versus-reorder query might still list them. |
| **Demand-planning reorder points** | Either | If you use NetSuite demand planning to calculate dynamic reorder points, the threshold itself moves over time; the card reads whatever the current point is. |
| **Subsidiary filter** | Either | The card respects the dashboard subsidiary filter; a view over all subs reads higher. |

There is a real upstream dependency on the commerce connectors here, even though there is no dollar-for-dollar cross-connector reconciliation. Available quantity is only correct if NetSuite's inventory reflects reality, and on commerce businesses inventory is constantly decremented by orders flowing in from the storefront. If the commerce-to-NetSuite inventory sync drifts, available quantity in NetSuite can be wrong, which means the reorder trigger fires too early or too late. So when this card behaves oddly, items below reorder that the warehouse insists are well stocked, or items stocking out that never appeared here, check [ERP vs ecom Inventory Variance](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/erp-vs-ecom-inventory-variance) and [Inventory Sync Drift](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/inventory-sync-drift) first. A sync gap corrupting available quantity is a common reason this card disagrees with what the warehouse team sees on the floor.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**What is a reorder point and where does it come from?**
The reorder point is the inventory level you configure in NetSuite at which an item should be reordered to avoid running out. It is usually sized to cover expected demand over the supplier lead time plus a safety buffer. It can be entered manually on the item record or calculated dynamically by NetSuite demand planning. This card reads whatever the current reorder point is and counts items whose available quantity has fallen below it.

**Why does an item with stock on the shelf show as below reorder?**
Because the card uses available quantity, not raw on-hand. Available nets what is already committed to open orders and what is already on order against the physical on-hand. An item can have boxes on the shelf that are nearly all committed to existing sales orders, so the quantity free for new demand is below the reorder point. This is correct and forward-looking; you cannot dismiss an item by checking the shelf, you have to check how much is already spoken for.

**An item is below reorder but already on a PO, is that a problem?**
No, that is the system working. The threshold triggered and a purchase order was raised, so replenishment is on its way. The card may still count it as below reorder (the condition is true) but it should be read as covered. The items that need action are those below reorder with no PO. Always separate covered from uncovered; the uncovered count is the real work queue.

**What does a rising count usually mean?**
One of three things: demand genuinely accelerating, supplier lead times stretching (which makes existing reorder points too low), or reorder points that have drifted out of date. The first is good news that needs faster buying; the second means the reorder points themselves should be raised; the third means the configuration needs maintenance. The count alone does not tell you which, so pair it with lead-time and demand context from the operations team.

**Multi-location and transfers, how do they affect this card?**
Reorder points are often set per location, so an item can be below reorder at one warehouse and fine at another, and the card counts per location. Inter-warehouse transfers reduce available quantity at the sending location and can push items below reorder there even with no sales change. A spike at one location should prompt the question whether it came from sales, a transfer, or a reorder-point change, because the right fix differs.

**OneWorld and per-subsidiary inventory, what should I watch for?**
On OneWorld, items, locations, and reorder points can be defined per subsidiary, so the count is attributable to a sub and a warehouse. The card respects the subsidiary filter. The most useful pattern is comparing the covered-versus-uncovered ratio across subs and locations, because that reveals which entity's replenishment discipline or reorder-point configuration is weakest, independent of raw volume.

**B2B versus DTC, does the reading differ?**
Yes. DTC demand is spiky and fast, so DTC SKUs breach reorder frequently and the queue churns quickly; the priority is speed of replenishment. B2B demand is lumpier, with large customer-allocated orders that can drop available quantity sharply in one transaction, so a B2B item can go from comfortable to below reorder in a single large SO. B2B buyers often hold higher safety stock for key accounts, so a below-reorder B2B item can be more urgent than the count alone suggests.

**Why does a sync gap with my storefront distort this card?**
Because available quantity is only as accurate as NetSuite's inventory, which is decremented by orders flowing in from the storefront. If the commerce-to-NetSuite sync drifts, available quantity is wrong, so the reorder trigger fires too early or too late. Items can appear below reorder that are actually well stocked, or stock out without ever appearing here. Check the inventory variance and sync-drift cards when this card disagrees with the warehouse floor.

**Does this card raise purchase orders for me?**
No. It is a detection and reporting card; it counts items that have crossed their threshold so the buying team can act. NetSuite's own order-items and replenishment tooling is where POs are generated. The card's job is to make the work queue visible and alert when it is non-empty, not to automate the purchasing decision, which depends on lead times, MOQs, supplier terms, and cash that live outside this single count.

**Should the alert really fire at greater than zero?**
For many businesses, yes, because any uncovered item below reorder is a future stockout if ignored. But if a small standing queue is normal for you, because you batch purchasing weekly and tolerate a few items dipping below reorder between buys, tune the threshold so the card fires only when the queue exceeds your normal cadence. The default is conservative precisely so nothing slips silently; calibrate it to your buying rhythm.

**How does this compare to reorder management in other ERPs?**
Every inventory-capable ERP has a reorder-point or min/max concept, but the sophistication differs. NetSuite supports manual reorder points and dynamic demand-planning-calculated points, evaluated per location. Oracle, SAP, and Microsoft Dynamics have comparable min/max and MRP-driven replenishment under different names, some with deeper supply-planning engines. The card normalises NetSuite's available-versus-reorder condition into a single live count with a covered-versus-uncovered breakdown, so the reading is consistent regardless of how the underlying ERP models replenishment, while the buying action stays in NetSuite.
