> Active Vendors: the count of vendor master records with recent transaction activity, your live supplier base rather than the full vendor list.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> Your vendor master list grows forever, because old suppliers rarely get deleted. This card cuts through that and counts only the vendors you are actually transacting with right now, the ones receiving purchase orders, bills, or payments. It is the working size of your supply base, which matters for spend concentration, AP workload, and vendor-management focus.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | Distinct vendor records flagged active that have at least one recent transaction (purchase order, vendor bill, vendor payment, or credit) within the lookback. Inactive and never-transacted vendor records are excluded. |
| **Tax treatment** | n/a. Vendor counts are operational; tax sits on the underlying bills and the vendor's tax registration, not on the count. |
| **Currency** | n/a for the count itself. Associated spend is shown in reporting currency when drilled. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Respects dashboard filter. On OneWorld, the same legal vendor can exist as separate records per subsidiary, so the count reflects vendor-records-in-scope, not unique legal entities. |
| **Time window** | `RT` (real-time snapshot of currently active vendors). |
| **Alert trigger** | None. This card is informational; it is context for spend and AP cards, not an alarm. |
| **Roles** | owner, operations, finance |
| **Chart type** | Single KPI number with period-over-period delta. |
| **Why it matters** | Tells you the real size of your supply base, drives AP workload planning, and frames vendor-concentration and on-time-payment analysis. |
{/* /HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}

## Calculation

The card scans the vendor master and counts records that are both flagged active and have transactional activity within the lookback period. Transactional activity means the vendor appears on a recent purchase order, vendor bill, vendor payment, or vendor credit. Vendors that exist in the master but have not transacted recently, and vendors marked inactive, are excluded so the number reflects the working supply base rather than the full historical list. The logic is deliberately a count of records-in-scope, not a deduplicated count of legal entities, because NetSuite stores vendors per subsidiary and the operational question is usually "how many vendor records are my AP team handling" rather than "how many distinct companies do we buy from worldwide".

Because the card is informational, it does not apply a threshold or a value weighting. It is designed to sit alongside spend, AP aging, and vendor-payment cards as the denominator that gives those metrics meaning. A 12 percent rise in AP balance reads very differently if the active-vendor count also rose 12 percent (you are simply buying from more suppliers) versus if it stayed flat (your existing suppliers are extending you more credit or you are paying more slowly). The count is the context that turns a spend number into a story.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A growing outdoor-gear retailer on NetSuite OneWorld. Two subsidiaries: a US parent (USD) and a Canadian sub (CAD). Annual revenue roughly $40M. Real-time snapshot taken 18 Jun 26. Reporting currency USD. These figures are illustrative.

| Vendor segment | Records in master | Active (transacted recently) | Share of recent spend |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core product suppliers | 60 | 48 | 78% |
| Logistics and freight | 22 | 14 | 11% |
| Marketing and agencies | 35 | 18 | 6% |
| Facilities and operations | 40 | 12 | 3% |
| Professional services | 28 | 9 | 2% |
| One-off / dormant | 310 | 0 | 0% |
| **Total** | **495** | **101 (this card)** | **100%** |

Five things to notice:

1. **The vendor master holds 495 records but only 101 are active, so 80 percent of the list is noise.** This is the single most useful thing the card reveals on a first read. Vendor masters accumulate dormant records over years, because finance teams are reluctant to delete a vendor in case an old bill or a 1099 needs to be reissued. The result is a list that is mostly archaeology. The active-vendor count strips that away and shows the working supply base, which is the number that should drive AP staffing, vendor-review meetings, and supplier-risk monitoring. Reporting against 495 makes every per-vendor metric look diluted; reporting against 101 makes them actionable.

2. **Spend concentration is the immediate follow-on insight: 48 core product suppliers carry 78 percent of spend.** The count alone is context, but paired with spend it exposes how dependent the business is on a small group. If a handful of those 48 suppliers represent the bulk of the 78 percent, a single supplier failure or price hike is a material risk. The active-vendor count is the denominator that lets you compute concentration honestly. Computing concentration against the full 495 would understate it badly, because the 394 dormant records contribute nothing and dilute the percentages into meaninglessness.

3. **A rising active-vendor count is not automatically good.** Growth in the supply base can mean the business is healthily expanding its sourcing, or it can mean vendor sprawl: buyers opening new suppliers instead of consolidating spend with existing ones, which weakens negotiating leverage and multiplies AP workload. The card surfaces the trend; the interpretation depends on whether spend per vendor is rising (consolidation, good) or falling (sprawl, usually bad). Pairing the count with average spend per active vendor turns an ambiguous number into a clear procurement signal.

4. **On OneWorld, the same supplier counted twice is a real and common artefact.** A logistics company that bills both the US parent and the Canadian sub will usually exist as two vendor records, one per subsidiary, because NetSuite scopes vendors by subsidiary for AP and tax purposes. The active-vendor count therefore reflects records, not unique legal entities, and a multi-subsidiary group will show a higher count than a single-entity business buying from the exact same set of suppliers. This matters when you benchmark vendor count across business units; always compare like-for-like entity structures, and use the drill-down to identify shared suppliers that could be negotiated as a single global account.

5. **The dormant records are a cleanup and risk opportunity hiding in plain sight.** 394 inactive or never-transacted vendor records are not just clutter; each one is a potential fraud and data-integrity surface. Dormant vendor records with live bank details are a classic vector for payment-redirection fraud, and stale records make duplicate-vendor errors more likely (two records for the same supplier, payments split, discounts missed). The card does not police this directly, but the gap between master size and active count is the prompt for a periodic vendor-master cleanup. Most finance teams never look at this gap until an audit forces it; surfacing it continuously turns a year-end chore into a managed hygiene metric.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

The vendor master and its transaction history are native, so this card is straightforward to triangulate against NetSuite's own lists and reports:

> **Lists → Relationships → Vendors** (filter on the active flag to see the inactive-vs-active split of the full master)
> **Reports → Purchases → Vendor Register** (vendors with purchasing activity in the period; close to the card's active definition)
> **Reports → Vendors/Payables → A/P Register** (vendors with bill or payment activity)
> **Saved search**: a Vendor saved search filtered to Inactive is false, with a transaction-count or last-transaction-date column over the lookback, grouped by subsidiary; this is the population the card counts
> **SuiteAnalytics workbook**: a Vendors workbook joined to Transactions, with a "transacted in last N days" filter, gives the per-subsidiary active count the card aggregates

The headline difference is the activity filter. The native Vendors list shows everyone who is not flagged inactive, which is far more than the card's count, because plenty of active-flagged vendors have not transacted in months. The card only counts vendors that are both active and recently transacting.

**Why our number may legitimately differ from a NetSuite vendor list:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Activity filter** | Card is lower | The native Vendors list counts everyone not flagged inactive; the card additionally requires recent transaction activity. |
| **Lookback window** | Either | The definition of "recent" depends on the lookback; a saved search with a different date range will return a different count. |
| **Subsidiary scoping** | Either | The card respects the dashboard filter; the same legal vendor with records under multiple subs counts once per record in scope. |
| **Transaction types included** | Either | The card counts POs, bills, payments, and credits; a register that only counts bills will be lower, one that includes quotes or requests could be higher. |
| **Inactive flag timing** | Either | A vendor recently flagged inactive but with activity earlier in the lookback may be counted differently by the card and a point-in-time list. |
| **Duplicate vendor records** | Card may be higher | Two records for the same supplier both count if both are active; a deduplicated analysis would merge them. |
| **One-time vendors** | Either | Some teams use a generic "one-time vendor" record for ad-hoc purchases; this can inflate or mask the true active count depending on policy. |

There is no cross-connector reconciliation for this card. The active-vendor base is a NetSuite supply-side concept with no counterpart in commerce or ad platforms, so the only valid comparison is against NetSuite's own vendor and AP data.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**What makes a vendor "active" for this card?**
Two conditions together: the vendor record is flagged active in NetSuite, and it has at least one recent transaction (purchase order, bill, payment, or credit) within the lookback. A vendor that is flagged active but has not transacted in a long time is excluded, which is the whole point: the card measures the working supply base, not the historical list.

**Why is the count so much lower than my full vendor list?**
Because vendor masters accumulate dormant records for years. Finance teams rarely delete vendors, in case an old bill, refund, or tax form needs reissuing. The full list is mostly archaeology. The active count strips it back to the suppliers you are actually doing business with now.

**Is a rising active-vendor count good or bad?**
It depends. Healthy expansion of sourcing raises it for good reasons. Vendor sprawl (buyers opening new suppliers instead of consolidating) also raises it, and that weakens negotiating leverage and multiplies AP work. Read the count alongside average spend per active vendor: rising spend per vendor suggests consolidation, falling spend per vendor suggests sprawl.

**How does OneWorld change the count?**
The same legal supplier often exists as separate vendor records per subsidiary, because NetSuite scopes vendors by subsidiary for AP and tax. So a multi-subsidiary group shows a higher count than a single entity buying from the identical set of suppliers. The card counts records in scope, not unique companies. Use the drill-down to spot shared suppliers that could be negotiated as one global account.

**Does this count include 1099 / tax-reporting vendors only?**
No. It counts all active, recently transacting vendors regardless of tax-form status. 1099 (US) or equivalent tax-reporting vendors are a subset you can filter to in NetSuite, but the card's headline is the full active base.

**Why is this informational and not an alert?**
Because there is no universal "right" number of vendors. A 101-vendor count is healthy for one business and a problem for another. The card exists to give context to spend, AP, and payment cards, which is far more useful as a denominator than as an alarm. If you want alerting, the actionable thresholds live on the spend and payment cards, not on the count.

**B2B vs DTC, does the reading differ?**
The mechanism is identical, but B2B and wholesale operations typically run a larger, more complex supply base (multiple component suppliers, contract manufacturers, freight forwarders, customs brokers) than a lean DTC brand sourcing finished goods from a handful of suppliers. Calibrate expectations to your model rather than to a generic benchmark.

**Does multi-currency affect the count?**
Not the count itself; a vendor is counted regardless of the currency it bills in. Currency only matters when you drill into associated spend, which is shown in the reporting currency with the usual FX translation. The headline count is currency-agnostic.

**What about duplicate vendor records?**
If two records exist for the same supplier and both are active and transacting, both count. That is itself a useful finding, because duplicate vendors split payments, miss volume discounts, and complicate reconciliation. The gap between the active count and the true number of distinct suppliers is a prompt for a vendor-master cleanup.

**How do dormant vendor records create risk?**
Dormant records with live bank details are a known vector for payment-redirection fraud, and stale records increase the chance of duplicate-vendor errors. The card does not police this directly, but the gap between the master size and the active count is the signal that a periodic cleanup is overdue.

**Does the active count drive AP workload?**
Strongly. AP effort scales with the number of vendors being actively billed and paid, not with the size of the dormant master. Using the active count to plan AP staffing is far more accurate than using the full vendor list, which would massively overstate the workload.

**How does this compare to other ERPs?**
Every ERP keeps a vendor or supplier master and most distinguish active from inactive, but the "recently transacting" definition is a Vortex IQ refinement layered on top of NetSuite's data. Oracle and SAP have richer supplier-lifecycle states (qualified, blocked, archived) that some businesses use to manage the same idea natively; the card normalises this into a single working-supply-base number that reads the same way across connectors.

**Does period close affect this card?**
Minimally. The card is a real-time snapshot of currently active vendors, not a period-bound financial number. A close in progress does not change which vendors are active. It only matters if you drill into associated period spend, which follows the usual close mechanics.
