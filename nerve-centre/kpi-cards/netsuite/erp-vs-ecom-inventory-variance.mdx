> A single percentage gauge showing how far your NetSuite on-hand has drifted from what your storefronts say is available, rolled up across every SKU. The headline rollup of the per-SKU drift table.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> One aggregate percentage measuring how far apart your two sources of inventory truth have drifted: NetSuite (the system of record) and your commerce platforms (what customers can actually buy). It is the rollup of the per-SKU drift detail, compressed into a single oversell-risk gauge. Above 5% means a meaningful slice of your catalogue is out of sync, and someone is either overselling stock you do not have or hiding stock you do.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | Aggregate absolute divergence between NetSuite available on-hand and commerce-platform availability across all synced SKUs, expressed as a percentage of total units (or value-weighted, per workspace config). |
| **Direction** | Variance is unsigned at the headline; the per-SKU detail shows whether each SKU is over- or under-stated on the storefront. |
| **Currency** | n/a. The headline is a unit-based percentage; a value-weighted variant reads in reporting currency. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Respects dashboard filter. Locations roll up to their owning subsidiary on OneWorld. |
| **Time window** | `RT/24H` (real-time variance, with a 24-hour trailing view to distinguish a spike from a persistent drift) |
| **Alert trigger** | Aggregate variance greater than 5% |
| **Roles** | owner, finance, operations |
| **Chart type** | Gauge (percentage), banded green/amber/red, with a count of affected SKUs beneath |
{/* /HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}

## Calculation

Calculated automatically from your NetSuite data joined to your commerce connectors. For every SKU that exists in both NetSuite and a commerce platform, the card compares NetSuite available on-hand against the availability the storefront is advertising, takes the absolute difference, and aggregates those differences into one percentage across the whole synced catalogue. The headline is unsigned because it measures total drift in both directions; the per-SKU detail preserves the sign so you can tell oversell risk (storefront higher than ERP) apart from hidden stock (storefront lower than ERP).

The 24-hour trailing component exists to separate a transient spike (a sync that briefly lagged and then caught up) from a persistent structural drift (a feed that is genuinely broken). The headline alert fires above 5%, but the action is always driven down into the per-SKU drift table, because a 6% aggregate caused by one giant SKU is a very different problem from 6% spread thinly across hundreds. See the At a glance summary above and the worked example below.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A US apparel-and-accessories brand on NetSuite OneWorld. US parent (USD) sells DTC through Shopify and wholesale through a B2B portal; a UK sub (GBP) sells through BigCommerce. Around 4,200 active SKUs synced across platforms. Reading taken 12 Jun 26, with a 24-hour trailing view. Reporting currency USD.

| Measure | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Synced SKUs (NetSuite and commerce) | 4,200 |
| SKUs within tolerance (variance under threshold) | 3,918 |
| SKUs over-stated on storefront (oversell risk) | 174 |
| SKUs under-stated on storefront (hidden stock) | 108 |
| Aggregate unit variance | 6.4% |
| Largest single-SKU contribution | 1.1% (one hero SKU, 900 units out) |
| **ERP-vs-Ecom Inventory Variance % (this card)** | **6.4%** |

Five things to notice:

1. **6.4% is above the 5% alert line, which means oversell risk is live right now, not theoretical.** The 174 SKUs over-stated on the storefront are items where a customer can place an order for stock NetSuite says you do not have. Every one of those is a potential oversell: a cancelled order, a back-order email, a refund, and a customer who may not come back. Aggregate variance is the smoke; the 174 over-stated SKUs are the fire. The reason this is a hero card with a tight 5% threshold is that oversell damage is immediate and reputational, unlike most accounting drift which surfaces slowly at close. The moment this gauge crosses red, the storefront is making promises the warehouse cannot keep.

2. **The gauge is a rollup, and its entire job is to hand you off to the SKU-level detail.** A single 6.4% number is not actionable on its own; you cannot fix a percentage. The card exists to detect and triage, then route you into the [SKUs with NetSuite-vs-Ecom Inventory Drift](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/inventory-sync-drift) detail table, where each of the 282 affected SKUs is listed with its NetSuite count, its commerce count, the gap and the location. The aggregate gauge answers "is there a problem and how big"; the drift table answers "which SKUs and by how much". Reading the gauge without drilling into the table is like reading a fire alarm and never checking which floor.

3. **One hero SKU contributing 1.1% of the entire 6.4% is the highest-leverage fix on the board.** When a single SKU is 900 units out of sync, fixing that one feed mapping drops the aggregate from 6.4% toward 5.3% in a single action. This is the value of the value-weighted view: variance is rarely spread evenly, and a Pareto pattern usually means a handful of high-velocity SKUs cause most of the risk. The operating discipline is to sort the drift table by contribution, fix the top few, and watch the gauge fall. Spreading attention evenly across all 282 SKUs wastes effort on long-tail items that barely move the headline or the business.

4. **The 108 under-stated SKUs are the quieter, costlier problem hiding behind the oversell headline.** When the storefront shows less stock than NetSuite holds, you are not at oversell risk, you are losing sales you could have made. Customers see "out of stock" or low-availability nudges on items you actually have in the warehouse, so they buy elsewhere or buy a competitor's product. This costs nothing to refund and generates no angry email, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed for months. The 174 oversell SKUs scream; the 108 hidden-stock SKUs are silent lost revenue. The signed per-SKU detail is what lets you see both halves of the drift, where the aggregate gauge only shows the total.

5. **On OneWorld, the consolidated 6.4% can be one healthy sub plus one broken feed.** If the US Shopify sync is clean at 2% and the UK BigCommerce sync is broken at 14%, the blended catalogue lands around 6.4% and the gauge looks like a uniformly mediocre sync when in fact one platform is fine and the other is failing. Read the gauge per subsidiary and per platform using the dashboard filter, because the fix lives at the platform-and-sub level: a single broken BigCommerce feed mapping on the UK sub is one configuration change, not a catalogue-wide problem. The consolidated number tells the executive there is drift; the per-platform read tells operations which integration to fix.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

NetSuite cannot produce this number on its own, because NetSuite cannot see the commerce side of the comparison. What you can do is establish the NetSuite half of the equation natively, then compare it against the storefront export. The NetSuite-side views:

> **Reports → Inventory → Inventory Activity Detail** (the authoritative NetSuite available on-hand, the system-of-record half)
> **Lists → Accounting → Items** with the on-hand and available-by-location sublist (per-SKU, per-location availability)
> **Reports → Inventory → Inventory Availability** (committed versus available, which matters because storefronts usually publish available, not gross on-hand)
> **Saved search**: an item saved search returning `Available` per SKU per location, exported and compared against the commerce platform's available-to-sell export
> On the commerce side, the platform's own inventory or availability export (Shopify inventory levels, BigCommerce inventory, Adobe Commerce MSI source quantities)

The reconciliation is a two-file join: NetSuite available per SKU against commerce available per SKU. The card does this continuously; doing it manually is a periodic spreadsheet exercise that is exactly what this gauge automates.

**Why our number may legitimately differ from a manual NetSuite-vs-storefront comparison:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Available vs on-hand** | Either | NetSuite distinguishes gross on-hand, committed and available. Storefronts publish available. If your manual comparison uses on-hand rather than available, it will show drift the card does not, because the card compares like for like. |
| **Sync latency window** | Either | A feed that updates every few minutes will momentarily lag NetSuite. The 24-hour trailing view distinguishes this transient lag from a persistent break; a single-instant manual snapshot cannot. |
| **Location mapping** | Either | If a SKU is fulfilled from two NetSuite locations but the storefront maps to one, the available figure differs. The card respects the configured location mapping; a naive comparison may not. |
| **Safety-stock buffers** | Either | Many merchants deliberately publish less than full availability (a buffer) to avoid oversell. That intentional gap reads as under-statement; the card can be configured to treat a buffer as expected rather than drift. |
| **Pre-orders and back-orders** | Either | Storefronts may advertise availability for pre-order SKUs that NetSuite shows as zero on-hand. This is intended over-statement, not a sync failure. |
| **Bundles and kits** | Either | A bundle's storefront availability is derived from component on-hand; if the bundle logic differs between systems the variance is computed, not real. |
| **Value-weighted vs unit-weighted** | Either | The headline can be unit-weighted or value-weighted per workspace; a manual comparison done the other way will land on a different percentage. |
| **Catalogue scope** | Either | The card only compares SKUs present in both systems; SKUs that exist in only one are a separate problem and excluded from the variance percentage. |

**Cross-connector reconciliation, the killer cross-channel finding:**

This card is inherently cross-connector, because its entire definition is a join between NetSuite and the commerce platforms. No single system can produce it. NetSuite knows what you have; the storefront knows what you are promising; only the join knows whether the two agree.

| Card | Expected relationship | What the comparison reveals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [SKUs with NetSuite-vs-Ecom Inventory Drift](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/inventory-sync-drift) | Rollup-to-detail | This gauge is the sum; the drift table is the line items. They must reconcile: the aggregate is the weighted roll-up of the per-SKU gaps. |

The killer cross-channel finding is that oversell risk is a join, not a number that lives in either system. NetSuite cannot see that the storefront is advertising 900 units it does not have, because NetSuite has no view of the storefront. The storefront cannot see that NetSuite has been depleted, because the feed broke. Only Vortex IQ, holding both in one graph, can compute the divergence in real time and fire the gauge red the moment the feed drifts. On a typical mid-market account this catches an oversell incident hours or days before it would have surfaced as a wave of cancelled orders and refunds, which is the difference between a quiet feed fix and a customer-trust event.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**Why is the headline unsigned when over- and under-statement are such different problems?**
Because the headline's job is to answer "how out of sync is my catalogue overall", and total drift in either direction degrades trust in your inventory data. An unsigned aggregate is the right summary for the executive gauge. But the card never asks you to act on the unsigned number: the per-SKU drift detail preserves the sign, so the operator immediately sees the 174 oversell SKUs (storefront higher than ERP) apart from the 108 hidden-stock SKUs (storefront lower than ERP). Use the gauge to know there is drift; use the signed detail to know which kind.

**What is the difference between on-hand, committed and available, and which does this use?**
On-hand is everything physically in the building. Committed is what is already promised to open orders. Available is on-hand minus committed, which is what you can actually sell to a new customer. Storefronts publish available, so the card compares NetSuite available against commerce available. If you ever see drift that looks wrong, the first check is whether the comparison is on the same basis: comparing NetSuite on-hand against storefront available will always show a gap equal to your committed quantity, which is not real drift.

**My storefront deliberately shows a safety-stock buffer. Does the card flag that as drift?**
Only if it is not configured to expect the buffer. Many merchants intentionally publish less than full availability to avoid oversell, which mathematically looks like under-statement. The card can be told that a buffer of a certain size is expected, so a deliberate buffer does not generate false drift. Confirm your buffer policy is reflected in the workspace config, otherwise every buffered SKU will read as hidden stock and inflate the gauge.

**OneWorld and subsidiaries, how should I read drift across entities?**
Read it per subsidiary and per platform, not just consolidated. The consolidated gauge blends every platform on every sub, so one broken feed on one sub can drag the whole number while everything else is clean. Because the fix lives at the platform-and-sub level (a specific feed mapping on a specific entity), the per-sub, per-platform read is what tells you where to point the wrench. The consolidated number is the alarm; the filtered read is the address.

**FX and multi-currency, does it affect this card?**
Not the unit-weighted headline, because units do not have a currency. If you run the value-weighted variant, then yes, the dollar weighting of each SKU's drift translates to reporting currency, so a large FX swing can nudge the value-weighted gauge even when not a single unit changed. For pure oversell-risk monitoring, the unit-weighted view is cleaner because it is currency-agnostic; the value-weighted view is for prioritising fixes by financial exposure.

**B2B versus DTC, does drift mean the same thing?**
The mechanics are identical but the consequences differ. On DTC, a drifting SKU leads directly to a consumer oversell, a refund and a reputational hit, all within hours. On B2B, orders often pass through a sales rep or a credit-approval gate before fulfilment, so a drift has a chance to be caught before it becomes a broken promise. That said, a B2B oversell is often larger per line (bulk quantities), so a single drifting B2B SKU can be a bigger dollar event than many DTC oversells. Read DTC drift for speed and B2B drift for size.

**How do pre-orders and back-orders interact with the gauge?**
They are intentional over-statement. A pre-order SKU advertises availability the warehouse does not yet hold, by design. If the card is not told these SKUs are pre-order, it will read them as oversell risk and inflate the gauge with false positives. Mark pre-order and back-order SKUs in the config so the card excludes the planned over-statement and only flags the accidental kind. This is one of the most common sources of a falsely high reading on brands that run drops or pre-sells.

**Why does the gauge sometimes spike and then settle within the hour?**
Because feeds sync on a cadence, not instantaneously. A large receipt or a bulk fulfilment in NetSuite creates a momentary gap until the next feed cycle pushes the new availability to the storefront. The 24-hour trailing view exists precisely to distinguish this transient lag from a persistent break: a spike that settles is normal sync latency, while a spike that holds for hours is a broken feed. Do not chase every transient blip; act when the trailing view stays elevated.

**Does period close affect inventory variance?**
Indirectly. During close, physical-count adjustments and costing recalculations post in NetSuite, which can move the NetSuite half of the comparison faster than the storefront feed can follow. A brief rise in variance during count-reconciliation is a workload artefact. If the elevated variance persists after counts are posted and feeds have cycled, it is a real sync problem, not a close artefact.

**Can SuiteScript or a scheduled script cause drift on its own?**
Yes. If a SuiteScript that adjusts inventory or a scheduled fulfilment script fails or runs late, NetSuite availability changes without the storefront being notified, opening a gap. This ties the inventory-variance gauge to the integration-health side of the [NetSuite Health Score](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/netsuite-health-score): a script failure can show up as both an integration-pillar drop and an inventory-variance spike. When variance jumps with no obvious receipt or sale behind it, check the script execution log.

**What about bundles, kits and assemblies?**
These are the trickiest cases, because a bundle's availability is derived from its component on-hand, and the derivation logic can differ between NetSuite and the storefront. A bundle can show drift that is actually a difference in how the two systems compute availability from the same components, not a real stock discrepancy. The per-SKU detail flags bundle SKUs so you can treat them separately; do not assume bundle drift means physical stock is wrong.

**NetSuite versus other ERPs, is this comparison ERP-specific?**
The concept is universal: any ERP-plus-commerce stack has two sources of inventory truth that can drift. The NetSuite specifics are the available-versus-on-hand model and the location structure that the card respects. On SAP S/4HANA the equivalent is available-to-promise against plant and storage-location stock; on Oracle ERP Cloud it is available-to-transact across inventory organisations. The cross-connector value is identical regardless of ERP: the variance only exists in the join, and the join only exists when one platform holds both systems together, which is the entire point of the Vortex IQ graph.
