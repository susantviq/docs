## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> A breakdown of how many transactions of each type NetSuite recorded over the period, shown as a donut. It is the shape of your account's activity: how much is automated ecommerce flow (cash sales, customer invoices), how much is purchasing (vendor bills), and how much is manual accounting (journal entries). The mix is more diagnostic than the total, because shifts in the proportions reveal whether your automation is scaling cleanly or whether manual workarounds are creeping in.

## Calculation

Calculated automatically from your NetSuite data. The card counts every posted transaction over the trailing 30-day window, groups them by NetSuite record type, and draws each type as a proportional segment of a donut. It is a count-based composition view, not a value view: a single large vendor bill and a tiny one each count as one transaction, so the donut measures activity volume by type rather than dollar weight.

There is no alert because there is no universally correct mix; the right composition depends entirely on your business model. A high-volume DTC brand is dominated by cash sales and fulfilments, while a B2B distributor leans toward customer invoices and payments. The value is in watching the mix move over time, because a shift in proportions is often the earliest visible sign of a process change, an automation breaking, or manual workarounds creeping in. See the At a glance summary above and the worked example below.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A US health-and-wellness brand on NetSuite OneWorld. US parent (USD) runs high-volume DTC through Shopify; a UK sub (GBP) runs a smaller B2B wholesale book. Custom SuiteScript posts marketplace settlement journals. Annual revenue ~$50M. 30-day window 13 May 26 to 12 Jun 26. Reporting currency USD.

| Transaction type | Count | Share | Read |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cash Sale | 18,400 | 58% | DTC ecommerce flow, the engine |
| Item Fulfilment | 7,900 | 25% | Shipments against cash sales and orders |
| Customer Invoice | 2,200 | 7% | B2B wholesale book |
| Customer Payment | 1,300 | 4% | B2B collections |
| Vendor Bill | 980 | 3% | Purchasing |
| Journal Entry | 870 | 3% | Manual accounting and settlement scripts |
| **Total transactions** | **31,650** | **100%** | |

Five things to notice:

1. **The donut is a fingerprint of your business model, and the first read is whether the shape matches what you are.** A 58% Cash Sale share with a big Item Fulfilment slice is exactly what a high-volume DTC brand should look like: the engine is automated ecommerce flow, and invoices and payments are a minor B2B sideline. If this same brand suddenly showed Customer Invoices at 40%, that would be a red flag worth investigating, because it would mean either a business-model shift or, more likely, that something is routing DTC orders down the invoice path instead of the cash-sale path. The card has no alert because the right shape is business-specific, but the wrong shape for your model is immediately visible to anyone who knows what the business is.

2. **A Cash Sale spike is the good kind of movement: it means ecommerce is scaling.** If the Cash Sale segment grows month over month, your DTC engine is selling more, your storefront-to-NetSuite integration is keeping up, and the automation is doing its job. This is the segment you want to grow, and watching it expand as a share of total transactions is a clean, real-time proxy for ecommerce momentum that does not require waiting for a revenue report. Pair it with the actual revenue cards to confirm the volume is translating to dollars, but the transaction-count view often moves first because it counts the event the moment it posts.

3. **A Journal Entry spike is the worrying kind of movement: it usually means manual-override creep.** Journal entries are how accountants correct, reclassify and adjust. A small, stable JE share is healthy and expected. A rising JE share is one of the earliest signs that something automated has broken and humans are patching the books by hand, or that a process has degraded to the point where manual intervention has become routine. The 3% here is healthy; if it climbed to 10% or 15%, the question is what stopped working that now requires hand-keyed corrections. This is the direct link to the [Manual Journal Entries as % of Total](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/manual-journal-entries-as-of-total) gauge, which tracks exactly this share against a 25% alert line.

4. **The mix is a count, not a value, and reading it as value is the most common mistake.** Vendor Bills are only 3% of transaction count, but they could easily be the largest slice by dollar value, because a single vendor bill for a bulk inventory purchase dwarfs thousands of small cash sales. The donut answers "what is my account doing", not "where is my money". A merchant who reads the 3% Vendor Bill slice as "purchasing is tiny" has misread the chart: purchasing is tiny in transaction count and potentially huge in dollars. Use this card for activity composition and process health; use the revenue and COGS cards for the dollar picture.

5. **On OneWorld, the consolidated donut blends two very different business models into one misleading shape.** The US parent is 58% Cash Sale (DTC), but the UK sub is a B2B wholesale book that is mostly Customer Invoices and Payments. Blended, the donut shows a DTC-dominated shape simply because the US volume is larger, which hides the fact that the UK sub has a completely different and equally valid composition. Read the donut per subsidiary using the dashboard filter when you want to understand each entity's process health, and read it consolidated only when you want the group-level activity picture. A consolidated donut on a mixed-model group is a blend, not a description of either business.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

This is a straightforward card to reconcile, because NetSuite groups transactions by type natively. The closest native views:

> **Transactions → Management → Transaction Saved Search** grouped by Type, summarised by count, with a date filter on the 30-day window (this is the most direct equivalent of the card)
> **Reports → Financial → Transaction Detail** filtered by type
> **Lists → Transactions** with a type filter applied (a quick per-type count)
> **Reports → Sales → Sales by Transaction Type** for the revenue-side types specifically
> **Saved search**: a transaction saved search with `Type` as the summary field and a count column, which is essentially what this card renders

The headline counts should tie out closely to a transaction saved search grouped by type over the same window. The most common reconciliation gap is posting versus non-posting transactions and which statuses are included, both covered below.

**Why our number may legitimately differ from a transaction saved search:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Posting vs non-posting** | Either | Some transaction types are non-posting (a Sales Order, a quote, an estimate). Whether the card counts non-posting types or only posting ones changes which segments appear and their sizes. |
| **Status filter** | Either | Voided, deleted and rejected transactions may or may not be included. A saved search that includes voided records will count more than a card that excludes them. |
| **Window boundaries** | Small | The trailing 30-day window and a saved-search date range may differ by where they cut the boundary day. |
| **Memorised and recurring transactions** | Either | A recurring transaction template versus its generated instances can be counted differently depending on configuration. |
| **Type granularity** | Either | NetSuite has fine-grained internal types; the card groups some related types into one segment for readability (for example grouping credit memo variants), so segment counts can be coarser than a raw type list. |
| **Subsidiary scope** | Either | Consolidated versus per-sub reads differ when one sub is much busier than another. |
| **Sub-transactions** | Either | An item fulfilment and a customer invoice generated from the same sales order are separate transactions; counting at the document level versus the event level changes totals. |

**Cross-connector reconciliation:**

The transaction-type mix is where commerce volume becomes NetSuite activity, so it is naturally cross-connector on the sales side. The commerce platforms show orders captured; NetSuite shows those orders as cash sales, invoices and fulfilments. The two should move together.

| Card | Expected relationship | What the comparison reveals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [shopify.orders](/nerve-centre/shopify/orders) | Should track the Cash Sale segment | A growing Shopify order count should grow the Cash Sale segment in step; a divergence means orders are captured but not posting as cash sales. |
| [bigcommerce.orders](/nerve-centre/bigcommerce/orders) | Should track sales-side segments | Same for BigCommerce; a flat NetSuite sales-type count against rising BC orders points at a posting gap. |
| [Commerce Orders Without a NetSuite SO](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/commerce-orders-without-netsuite-so) | Inverse signal | If the Cash Sale segment falls while commerce orders hold steady, this card is the early warning and the orders-without-SO card is the confirmation. |
| [Transaction Imbalances](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/transaction-imbalances) | Diagnostic pair | A type whose count drops alongside a rising imbalance count tells you the integration posting that type is failing. |

The killer cross-channel framing is that the Cash Sale segment is a real-time proxy for whether commerce volume is actually landing in the books. If Shopify shows 18,400 orders and the Cash Sale segment shows 18,400 cash sales, the integration is healthy and every order became a posting. If Shopify shows 18,400 orders and the Cash Sale segment shows 17,900, then 500 orders captured money on the storefront and never posted in NetSuite, which is exactly the kind of silent revenue gap that no single system can see. The commerce platform thinks the order is done; NetSuite never heard about it; only the join between the order count and the transaction-type count reveals the 500-order hole.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**Is the donut counting transactions or dollars?**
Counts, not dollars. Each transaction is one unit regardless of its value, so a one-dollar cash sale and a million-dollar vendor bill each add one to their segment. This is the most important thing to understand about the card: it measures activity composition, not financial weight. A small segment by count (like Vendor Bills) can be the largest by dollar value. Use this card for process and volume; use the revenue, COGS and AP cards for the money.

**What is a healthy mix?**
There is no universal answer, which is why there is no alert. A high-volume DTC brand is dominated by cash sales and fulfilments; a B2B distributor leans toward invoices and payments; a manufacturer carries more vendor bills and assembly builds. The right mix is whatever matches your business model. The signal is not the shape itself but a change in the shape, because a shift in proportions usually means a process changed, an automation broke, or a manual workaround crept in.

**Why is a Journal Entry spike a warning sign?**
Because journal entries are the manual-correction mechanism of accounting. A rising JE share means more of your books are being adjusted by hand, which usually points to an automated process that has stopped working, leaving humans to patch the result. A small, stable JE share is healthy; a climbing one is one of the earliest visible signs of control erosion. This card shows the JE segment in context; the [Manual Journal Entries as % of Total](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/manual-journal-entries-as-of-total) card isolates it and alerts above 25%.

**Why is a Cash Sale spike usually good?**
Because cash sales are the automated output of a healthy DTC engine. A growing Cash Sale segment means the storefront is selling more and the integration is converting those sales into NetSuite postings cleanly. It is a real-time proxy for ecommerce momentum that often moves before the formal revenue reports, because it counts the event as it posts. Confirm the volume is translating to dollars with the revenue cards, but a rising Cash Sale count is generally the shape of growth.

**OneWorld and subsidiaries, how should I read the mix across entities?**
Read it per subsidiary when you want to understand each entity's process, and consolidated only for the group activity picture. A consolidated donut on a group with mixed business models (a DTC sub and a B2B sub) blends two valid but very different shapes into one that describes neither. The larger sub dominates the consolidated donut purely on volume, which can hide a completely different and healthy composition on the smaller sub. The dashboard filter lets you separate them.

**FX and multi-currency, does it affect this card?**
No, because it is a count card. A transaction is one transaction regardless of its currency, so exchange rates do not change the donut at all. This is one of the few NetSuite cards that is entirely FX-immune. If you switch to a value-weighted analysis elsewhere, currency matters, but the transaction-type composition by count is currency-agnostic by construction.

**B2B versus DTC, does the mix read differently?**
Fundamentally yes, and it is the clearest example of model-specific shape. DTC is cash-sale and fulfilment heavy because customers pay at checkout and orders ship immediately. B2B is invoice and payment heavy because customers buy on terms and pay later. A hybrid business shows both patterns blended. The card is most useful when you read it against the expectation for your model: a DTC brand showing a large invoice segment, or a B2B distributor showing a large cash-sale segment, is a sign that orders are being routed down the wrong path.

**Does period close change the mix?**
Yes, temporarily. Close periods generate more journal entries (accruals, reversals, reclassifications) and sometimes more credit memos and adjustments, so the JE segment swells during close and settles afterward. A JE bump confined to close week is a workload artefact, not a control problem. A JE share that stays elevated after close completes is a real signal worth investigating.

**Can this card detect a broken integration?**
Often, yes, and earlier than most. If a transaction type that your integration normally posts (say, Cash Sales from Shopify) suddenly drops in count while the upstream commerce orders hold steady, the integration that creates that type has likely broken. The mix shift is the symptom; the orders-without-a-NetSuite-SO card and the imbalance card are the confirmations. Watching the composition is a cheap, continuous integration-health check that does not require instrumenting the integration itself.

**How does this relate to transaction imbalances?**
Closely. If a particular transaction type starts failing to post because the integration constructing it is malformed, you will often see that type's count fall here and the imbalance count rise simultaneously. Reading the two cards together localises the problem: the type tells you which business flow is affected, and the imbalance count tells you it is a posting defect rather than a volume drop. Together they point at the specific integration to fix.

**NetSuite versus other ERPs, is transaction-type mix a portable concept?**
Yes. Every transactional ERP records activity as typed documents, so SAP S/4HANA (document types), Oracle ERP Cloud (transaction types) and others all have an equivalent composition view. The NetSuite specifics are the record-type names (Cash Sale, Customer Invoice, Vendor Bill, Journal Entry) and the posting-versus-non-posting distinction. The diagnostic logic is universal: a healthy automated business has a stable, model-appropriate mix, and a shift in the proportions is an early signal of process or integration change regardless of which ERP records the transactions.
