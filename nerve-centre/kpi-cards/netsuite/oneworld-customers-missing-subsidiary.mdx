> OneWorld-account customer records without a Subsidiary assignment that break intercompany flows and revenue rule application.

## At a glance

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:tldr */}
> The number of customer records on a NetSuite OneWorld account that have no Subsidiary assigned. On OneWorld, Subsidiary is mandatory for almost every downstream financial process: a customer with no subsidiary cannot be invoiced cleanly, breaks intercompany flows, and prevents revenue-recognition rules from applying correctly. Even a single missing-subsidiary record is a structural data defect, which is why the alert trips above zero.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **What it counts** | Distinct customer records on a OneWorld account with no Subsidiary value assigned. |
| **Currency** | n/a (a count of records, not a value). |
| **Subsidiary scope** | By definition these records have no subsidiary; the card surfaces them across the whole OneWorld account. |
| **Time window** | `RT` (real-time count of currently unassigned records) |
| **Alert trigger** | Greater than 0 (any unassigned record on a OneWorld account) |
| **Roles** | owner, finance, operations |
| **Applies to** | OneWorld accounts only; not applicable to single-subsidiary NetSuite accounts. |
| **Excludes** | Inactive customer records in some configurations; non-customer entity types (vendors, partners). |

## Calculation

The card counts customer records on a OneWorld account where the Subsidiary field is empty. On OneWorld, every transactional entity is supposed to belong to a subsidiary, because the subsidiary determines which ledger a transaction posts to, which currency and tax rules apply, how intercompany eliminations work, and which revenue-recognition rules govern the customer's invoices. A record with no subsidiary is therefore an orphan in the consolidation structure, and the card simply tallies how many such orphans exist right now.

This is a real-time count, not a period flow, so a record appears the instant it is created or imported without a subsidiary and disappears the instant one is assigned. The alert is set above zero deliberately: unlike a count where a small number is tolerable, any missing-subsidiary record on OneWorld is a defect that will eventually surface as a failed invoice, a broken intercompany posting, or a revenue rule that did not apply. The card is informational about the count but its real job is to catch these orphans before they cause a downstream failure. The worked example below shows how unassigned records arise and the cascade of problems they create if left unfixed.

## Worked example

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:worked_example */}
A skincare brand on NetSuite OneWorld, selling DTC through Shopify in the US and EU and wholesale into pharmacies. Three subsidiaries: a US parent (USD), an EU sub based in Ireland (EUR), and a UK sub (GBP). Group revenue roughly $52M. New customers arrive from the Shopify integration, from a bulk import after a recent marketing-list acquisition, and from manual entry by the wholesale team. The card is read live on 22 Jun 26.

| Source of record | Records created (recent) | Missing subsidiary | Likely cause |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shopify integration (US) | 1,420 | 0 | Mapping sets US sub correctly |
| Shopify integration (EU) | 610 | 4 | EU region not mapped to Ireland sub |
| Bulk import (acquired list) | 300 | 7 | Import template omitted the subsidiary column |
| Manual wholesale entry | 18 | 1 | Rep saved record before selecting sub |
| **Total missing subsidiary (this card)** | | **12** | |

Five things to notice:

1. **At 12 the card is over its zero-tolerance alert, and the source split shows three independent root causes, not one.** Four EU Shopify buyers are missing a subsidiary because the integration's region-to-subsidiary mapping has a gap (EU orders are not being routed to the Ireland sub), seven came from a bulk import whose template simply lacked a subsidiary column, and one is a manual-entry slip. These are three different fixes: repair the integration mapping, re-run the import with the subsidiary populated, and coach the wholesale rep. The card's value is not the headline 12 but the decomposition that turns one alarm into three targeted actions, which is why even a small count is worth the drill-down rather than a blanket clean-up.

2. **A customer with no subsidiary cannot be invoiced cleanly, so each orphan is a sale waiting to fail at the worst moment.** On OneWorld the subsidiary determines which ledger an invoice posts to, so an invoice raised against a subsidiary-less customer either errors out, posts to a default that misattributes the revenue, or sits blocked. The four EU buyers are the dangerous ones here: they are active DTC customers placing orders right now, and the moment an order tries to invoice, it hits the missing-subsidiary wall. So this card is not a tidy-the-data nicety; it is an early warning that 12 customers are primed to break order-to-cash, with the four active EU buyers being the most urgent because they are transacting today.

3. **Missing subsidiary breaks intercompany flows, which is the failure that quietly corrupts consolidation.** When a customer belongs to a subsidiary, NetSuite knows which entity's books their transactions belong to and can handle any intercompany elimination correctly. A subsidiary-less customer cannot participate in that structure, so transactions involving them can fall outside the consolidation logic, leaving intercompany balances that do not net and a consolidated set of books that does not tie. This is the kind of defect that an auditor finds at year-end and that takes days to unwind, because by then the orphan records have generated months of mis-posted or unposted transactions. Catching the 12 records today prevents a consolidation headache later, which is the strategic case for the zero-tolerance alert.

4. **Revenue-recognition rules are scoped by subsidiary, so an unassigned customer can silently skip revenue rules entirely.** On OneWorld, revenue-recognition policies and ASC 606 rule application are configured per subsidiary, so a customer with no subsidiary may have their invoices fall through without the correct revenue schedule applied. The danger is silence: the invoice might still post, the cash might still arrive, but the revenue is recognised on the wrong basis or not deferred when it should be, which misstates the period. For a subscription-heavy skincare brand with deferred-revenue schedules, this is a real exposure, because the difference between recognising a 12-month subscription up front and deferring it across the year is material and only correct if the subsidiary, and therefore the revenue rule, is set. The card protects the integrity of revenue recognition by catching these records before they invoice.

5. **The fix is reassignment, not creation, and reassignment has consequences that a simple data patch does not.** Unlike an absent customer (who needs to be created), a missing-subsidiary customer already exists, so the remedy is to assign the correct subsidiary. But on a record that has already transacted, changing the subsidiary can ripple into existing transactions, intercompany relationships, and revenue schedules, so it is not always a free edit. For the four EU buyers and the seven import records, assigning the subsidiary early (before they transact, or right after) is clean; for any record that has already generated invoices, the reassignment needs a finance review of the downstream postings. This is why the card pairs with [Ecom Customers Absent from NetSuite](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/ecom-customers-absent-from-netsuite): the two failures look similar but the absent case is a create and the missing-subsidiary case is a reassign with side effects, and routing each to the right remedy is the whole point of keeping them on separate cards.

## Reconciling against NetSuite

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:reconcile */}
**Where to look in NetSuite:**

Subsidiary is a native, normally-required field on the customer record under OneWorld, so this card reconciles directly against a customer list filtered on an empty subsidiary. The card counts customer records with no subsidiary assigned. The closest native views to triangulate against:

> **Lists → Relationships → Customers** (add the Subsidiary column to the list view and filter or sort for blanks)
> **Customer record → Primary Information / Classification** (the Subsidiary field; on OneWorld this should never be empty for a transacting customer)
> **Saved search**: a Customer saved search with a filter Subsidiary is empty (or "none"), returning name, source, and creation date to localise the cause
> **Setup → Company → Subsidiaries** (the valid subsidiary set; confirms which assignments are available)
> **Setup → Integration → connector field mapping** (the region-to-subsidiary mapping that, when incomplete, produces unassigned commerce buyers)
> **System Notes on the customer record** (who created it and how, distinguishing import, integration, and manual-entry causes)

The most common reconciliation subtlety is that some customer records legitimately allow multiple subsidiaries (NetSuite's multi-subsidiary customer feature), where a customer is shared across subs with one primary. A record using that feature has a primary subsidiary even though it touches several, and should not count as missing. If the card and a naive blank-filter disagree, multi-subsidiary customers with a primary set are the usual reason: they are assigned, just to more than one sub.

**Why our number may legitimately differ from a native customer-list filter:**

| Reason | Direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Multi-subsidiary customers** | Card may read lower | A customer shared across subs with a primary subsidiary set is assigned, not missing; a naive "subsidiary contains one value" filter may misclassify them. |
| **Inactive records** | Card may read lower | The card excludes inactive customers by default; a raw list view may include long-dead records that were never assigned. |
| **Non-customer entities** | Card reads lower | The card counts customers; a broad entity filter that includes vendors, partners, or employees will read higher. |
| **As-of timing** | Either | The card is live; records are created and assigned continuously, so a list run minutes apart can differ. |
| **Default-subsidiary preferences** | Card may read lower | If the account sets a default subsidiary on creation, new records are auto-assigned and never appear; turning that off exposes the true gap. |
| **Prospect and lead stages** | Either | Records in prospect or lead stage may or may not require a subsidiary depending on configuration, shifting the count. |
| **Import error rows** | Card may read higher | Rows that imported without a subsidiary appear immediately; a list filtered by a stale saved search may not yet show them. |
| **Custom subsidiary fields** | Either | Some accounts use a custom field for subsidiary routing alongside the native one; the card reads the native field unless configured otherwise. |

Reconciliation is usually exact once multi-subsidiary customers and inactive records are accounted for. A gap almost always traces to the multi-subsidiary feature (assigned records misread as missing) or to a default-subsidiary preference masking the true count.

## Known limitations / merchant FAQs

{/* HUMAN_AUTHORED:faqs */}
**Why is the alert set above zero rather than a tolerance?**
Because on OneWorld there is no benign reason for a transacting customer to have no subsidiary. Unlike counts where a small number reflects normal lag, a missing-subsidiary record is a structural defect that will eventually break an invoice, an intercompany posting, or a revenue rule. The zero-tolerance alert reflects that every such record is a future failure waiting to happen, so the right target is genuinely zero, and any non-zero reading deserves a look.

**What exactly breaks when a customer has no subsidiary?**
Three things, in roughly increasing severity. First, invoicing: the subsidiary determines which ledger an invoice posts to, so an invoice against a subsidiary-less customer can error, misattribute, or stall. Second, intercompany flows: the customer cannot participate correctly in the consolidation and elimination structure, leaving balances that do not net. Third, revenue recognition: revenue rules are scoped by subsidiary, so an unassigned customer's invoices can skip the correct revenue schedule, misstating the period. The card exists to catch the record before any of these fire.

**How is this different from a customer being absent entirely?**
An absent customer (counted on [Ecom Customers Absent from NetSuite](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/ecom-customers-absent-from-netsuite)) has no NetSuite record at all and needs to be created. A missing-subsidiary customer already exists in NetSuite but has no subsidiary assigned and needs to be reassigned. The distinction matters because the remedies differ and reassignment carries side effects (on existing transactions, intercompany relationships, and revenue schedules) that simple creation does not. Keeping them on separate cards routes each problem to the right fix.

**Does this card apply to single-subsidiary accounts?**
No. On a single-subsidiary NetSuite account there is only one subsidiary, so the field is either implicit or trivially set, and the missing-subsidiary failure mode does not exist. This card is OneWorld-only. If you are on a single-sub account it will read zero and is effectively not applicable; the related [Ecom Customers Absent from NetSuite](/nerve-centre/kpi-cards/netsuite/ecom-customers-absent-from-netsuite) card still applies to you.

**Why might a customer legitimately touch multiple subsidiaries?**
NetSuite's multi-subsidiary customer feature lets one customer record be shared across several subsidiaries with one designated as primary, which is common for a global account that buys from more than one of your entities. Such a record is assigned (it has a primary subsidiary) and should not count as missing, even though it spans subs. If the card reads lower than a naive blank-filter, multi-subsidiary customers with a primary set are usually why. Confirm your configuration so genuinely-assigned shared customers are not miscounted.

**How do commerce integrations cause missing-subsidiary records?**
Through incomplete region-to-subsidiary mapping. When a Shopify, BigCommerce, or Adobe Commerce buyer is created in NetSuite, the integration should map their region or storefront to the correct subsidiary; if a region is unmapped (the EU gap in the worked example), buyers from that region arrive with no subsidiary. The fix is in the connector mapping, not in NetSuite, so a cluster of missing-subsidiary records from one channel points straight at a mapping gap for that channel's region.

**Does multi-currency or FX relate to this card?**
Indirectly but importantly. The subsidiary determines the customer's functional currency and the tax and revenue rules that apply, so a missing subsidiary means the system does not know which currency or rules to use. There is no FX number on this card (it is a count), but the consequence of leaving records unassigned is downstream currency and tax misapplication, which is part of why the defect is serious rather than cosmetic.

**Can reassigning a subsidiary on an existing customer cause problems?**
Yes, if the customer has already transacted. Changing the subsidiary on a record with existing invoices or intercompany relationships can ripple into those transactions and any revenue schedules, so it is not always a free edit. Assigning the subsidiary before the customer transacts is clean; reassigning after transactions exist needs a finance review of the affected postings. This is the practical reason to catch missing-subsidiary records early, while the fix is still a simple assignment.

**Does period close affect this card?**
Not directly; the count is live and independent of the close calendar. Indirectly, the finance team often sweeps for unassigned and orphaned records as part of close preparation, because consolidation cannot tie if customers are missing subsidiaries, so the count tends to be driven toward zero around close. A reading taken before close prep will generally show more orphans than one taken after.

**B2B versus DTC, where do these records come from?**
Both, but via different routes. DTC missing-subsidiary records usually come from commerce-integration mapping gaps (a region not routed to a sub) and from bulk imports. B2B missing-subsidiary records usually come from manual entry, where a rep saves a record before selecting the subsidiary. The source split on the card tells you which channel and process to fix, and in practice DTC clusters point at integration mappings while B2B singletons point at data-entry discipline.

**NetSuite versus other ERPs on entity-to-entity assignment?**
The concept of a customer belonging to a legal entity for consolidation is universal across multi-entity ERPs; Oracle ERP Cloud and SAP S/4HANA both require business-partner-to-company-code or legal-entity assignment for the same reasons (ledger routing, intercompany, revenue rules). NetSuite's OneWorld makes Subsidiary a first-class required field on the customer record, which makes the gap easy to detect, and that is exactly what this card does.
